## VIDEO 15 — How to Identify Your HOSA Event

- **Objective:** the learner can find their exact event, list its components (test / presentation /
  clinical skill / interview / portfolio), and say what to practice where.
- **Duration:** ~4 min. **Placement:** HOSA Module 0 / Event Navigator (pairs with benchmark lesson,
  doc 11).
- **Framing:** medium shot; insert of the Event Navigator UI once built (placeholder graphic until
  then).

**Script (spoken):**

"The biggest HOSA mistake happens before practice even starts: preparing for the wrong kind of
event. HOSA is not one format. It's a family of very different competitions — and your first job is
knowing exactly which one is yours.

[SCREEN: Written tests · Clinical skills · Presentations · Interviews · Team events · Emergency
preparedness]

Some events are written tests — Medical Terminology, for example, is currently a 50-question timed
exam. Some are hands-on clinical skills performed for evaluators. Some are presentations you prepare
in advance. Some are interviews with a judge. Some are team events.

One thing worth clearing up early, because it trips people up: **there isn't a separate 'patient
conversation' event.** Talking with a patient is something you get scored on *inside* a clinical skill
round — it's rows on that event's rating sheet — and separately in the interview events. So 'I want to
do the one where you talk to a patient' isn't an event you can sign up for. Find your event first;
we'll tell you whether communication is part of how it's scored.

Preparing a patient conversation for a written-test event, or memorizing flashcards for a skills
event, is wasted work. So, three questions, in order:

[SCREEN: 1. What is my exact event's name? 2. What components does it have? 3. What does its rating
sheet reward?]

One: the exact name, from your advisor or the official HOSA list — not 'something medical,' the
exact event.

Two: the components. Read your event's current official guideline — the real document, this year's
version — and list what the event actually contains: a test? a presentation? a skill demonstration?
an interview? A portfolio you submit in advance? Many events combine more than one.

[PAUSE — Q: Why does this year's guideline matter — why not last year's notes from a teammate?]

Because events change. Formats change, question counts change, rubrics change. Medical Terminology
changed its own test length recently. Last year's notes are a rumor; this year's guideline is the
rule.

Three: the rating sheet. It tells you exactly what earns points — which is exactly what to practice.

One honest boundary: some HOSA events involve hands-on clinical skills. An app can teach you the
knowledge and the communication around them — it cannot teach your hands. Those skills need
supervised, in-person practice with your instructor. CompeteReady will always tell you which parts
it can train and which parts need the lab."

**Practice prompt:** "In the Event Navigator, select your event and answer: which components does it
have, and which of them can you train here?"
**Retake instruction:** "Not sure of your exact event? Stop here and confirm it with your advisor
before doing anything else — that ten-minute conversation is worth more than a week of wrong
practice."
**Editing notes:** the component-type cards should appear one at a time; keep the three questions on
screen through the second half.

## VIDEO 16 — Where Patient Communication Lives in a HOSA Skill Event

⟨REFRAMED — H1⟩ *Draft V1 titled this "What Happens in a HOSA Scenario Event" and treated scenario
events as a category. **No standalone patient-conversation event was located in the reviewed official
corpus.** Patient communication is scored as rating-sheet rows **inside clinical skill rounds**, and
separately in the interview events.*

- **Objective:** the learner can say where communication sits inside a clinical skill event, who the
  people in the room are, and what their assigned role permits.
- **Duration:** ~4 min. **Placement:** HOSA Branch B, lesson B1 — gated on the Navigator identifying
  the learner's event as a clinical skill event.
- **Framing:** medium shot.

**Script (spoken):**

"If your event includes a clinical skill performance, some of what you're scored on isn't the skill at
all — it's how you talk to the person while you do it. Here's where that fits.

You'll get a scenario and a role. And the room usually has more people in it than beginners expect, so
know who's who:

[SCREEN: The judge · a live patient or actor · a manikin or training arm · your teammate]

The **judge** is often playing the professional you report to — the nurse, the pharmacist. When a
rating sheet says 'reported concerns to the nurse,' the nurse is the judge. Some events provide a
**live patient or an actor** for the noninvasive parts, where the guideline and equipment list say so.
And for invasive steps, medication administration, blood draws, injections, or resuscitation, the
patient is a **manikin, a training arm, or a medication trainer** — the specified simulation equipment.
Some events mix these within a single skill. Your event's current guideline and equipment list tell you
which — that's the only place to find out.

[SCREEN: READ SCENARIO → MY ROLE → THE PERSON + THEIR CONCERN → MY BOUNDARIES → PERFORM AND
COMMUNICATE → REPORT → CLOSE]

The single biggest beginner mistake: treating the talking part as a speech — delivering health facts
AT a person. Rating sheets that score communication reward something else: greeting and introducing
yourself, explaining what you're doing, using plain language, staying inside what your role is allowed
to do, and reporting concerns to the right professional.

[PAUSE — Q: A worried patient asks you something. What should your FIRST sentence do — inform or
acknowledge?]

Acknowledge. 'That's an understandable worry' lands before any information does, because an anxious
person can't absorb facts until they feel heard.

One honest note about that: **the greeting is a scored step on official rating sheets. Acknowledging
someone's feelings is CompeteReady's teaching — it's how you make the greeting human, not a separate
row you'll find on the sheet.** We'll always tell you which is which.

The arc inside the room: open warmly, in role. Acknowledge the concern. Ask one clarifying question
so you're answering their REAL worry. Respond in plain language, inside your role's boundaries.
Handle the follow-up worry — there's usually one. Check they understood. Give a next step: connect
them to the nurse, the pharmacist, the right professional. Close professionally.

[SCREEN: Verbalize where the rating sheet requires it — but talking is not doing]

A word on verbalizing, because there's advice going around that says just say everything out loud.
That's an oversimplification of the actual rule. **Some rating sheets require you to state actions or
observations — do that, because it's a scored step. But saying a step out loud does not replace
performing it when the equipment is in front of you and the action has to actually happen.** Your
current guideline and rating sheet decide which steps are which.

And the boundary rule that runs under all of it: your assigned role doesn't diagnose, doesn't promise
outcomes, doesn't guess. Saying 'let me bring the nurse who can answer that properly' is not a
failure — knowing your limits IS one of the scored skills.

Your event's exact structure, timing, and rating sheet come from its current official guideline —
always check it. And one boundary about this app: **we can train the knowledge and the communication.
We can't train your hands, and finishing this branch doesn't mean you're ready for the complete
event.** The physical skill needs in-person practice with your instructor — that's CompeteReady's
policy, not a HOSA rule."

**Practice prompt:** "Open your branch's practice scenario. Identify your role, the person, their real
concern, and one boundary you must keep — before writing any response."
**Retake instruction:** "If you jumped straight to 'what should I say,' go back — role, person,
concern, boundary FIRST. The response comes from those four."
**Editing notes:** arc graphic persists and highlights step-by-step; keep tone warm — this video
models the demeanor it teaches.

## VIDEO 17 — Patient Language Versus Professional Language

- **Objective:** the learner can produce both versions of the same finding: a plain, calm
  patient-facing sentence and a precise professional report.
- **Duration:** ~4 min. **Placement:** HOSA Branch B, lesson B4 (plain-language explanation; two-channel introduction).
- **Framing:** medium shot; two-column channel graphic persistent.

**Script (spoken):**

"Health professionals are bilingual. Not English and Spanish — patient language and professional
language. Same fact, two completely different sentences. Competitors who master both stand out
immediately.

[SCREEN: CompeteReady's framework — grounded in scored steps, not an official HOSA concept]

One label first, so you know what you're learning. **'Two channels' is our way of teaching this, not
official HOSA terminology.** What IS official is that rating sheets score explaining the skill to the
patient, and separately score reporting to the professional. Those two scored steps are what this
framework is built on. You won't find a row on any sheet that says 'empathy' or 'plain language' —
those are our standards for doing the scored steps well.

Take one term: tachycardia. A fast heart rate.

Channel one — the patient channel. You're a volunteer; an anxious patient asks why the monitor is
beeping.

'Your heart is beating faster than expected. I'm going to report that finding to the appropriate
healthcare professional, so they can take a proper look.'

[SCREEN: PATIENT CHANNEL — plain · calm · empathetic · no unexplained terms]

Plain words. Calm tone. No jargon dumped on a worried person. And notice what I did NOT say — I
didn't say what it means, whether it's dangerous, or what happens next medically. That's not my
role, and guessing would be a boundary violation, not kindness.

Channel two — the professional channel. Reporting the same finding to the nurse:

'The patient presents with tachycardia.'

[SCREEN: PROFESSIONAL CHANNEL — precise · concise · accurate · rubric-relevant]

Four words? Perfect. The professional channel is not warmer or longer — it's exact. The nurse needs
the precise term, fast.

[PAUSE — Q: Try it yourself with 'hypertension' — high blood pressure. Say the patient sentence out
loud, then the professional one.]

Here's a model. Patient channel: 'Your blood pressure reading is higher than the expected range. I'll
make sure the nurse sees this right away.' Professional: 'The patient presents with hypertension on
today's reading.'

The failure modes are crossing the channels. Jargon at the patient — 'you're presenting with
tachycardia, possibly SVT' — frightens and confuses. Vagueness at the professional — 'their heart
seems kind of fast?' — wastes the handoff. One fact, two audiences, two disciplined sentences.

Every term you learn in your study decks gets this second life. Knowing the word is the exam skill.
Switching channels with it is the event skill."

**Practice prompt:** "Two-channel drill: you get a term you've studied. Write both sentences —
patient-facing and professional. The rubric checks plain language on one side, precision on the
other, and boundaries on both."
**Retake instruction:** "If your patient sentence contains the medical term unexplained, or your
professional sentence contains 'kind of' — rewrite that side only."
**Editing notes:** two-column graphic stays up from first use; deliver patient-channel demos with
visibly warmer register (this is also a delivery model); end card disclaimer + 'educational example,
not medical guidance.'

## VIDEO 18 — Anatomy and Terminology in Character

- **Objective:** the learner can carry a studied anatomy/terminology fact through the full bridge:
  meaning → observation → patient explanation → professional report → permitted action.
- **Duration:** ~4 min. **Placement:** HOSA Knowledge-to-Performance Bridge intro (linked from
  Lessons 11–13).
- **Framing:** medium shot; bridge-chain graphic builds across the video.

**Script (spoken):**

"You've memorized hundreds of terms for your written test. Here's how a term stops being a flashcard
and starts being something you can USE in a scenario room.

The bridge has six links. Watch one term cross it: 'abrasion' — a scrape, a wound where skin is
rubbed away.

[SCREEN, building link by link: TERM → MEANING → WHAT I OBSERVE → PATIENT EXPLANATION →
PROFESSIONAL REPORT → PERMITTED ACTION]

Meaning: a surface wound from friction — skin scraped, not cut deep.

What I observe — and observe is the key word. In role, you don't diagnose; you notice: 'There's a
red, scraped area on the forearm, about the size of a coin.'

Patient explanation — plain channel, calm: 'You've got a scrape on your arm. I'll let the nurse know
so it gets properly cleaned and looked at.'

Professional report — precise channel: 'The patient has an abrasion on the left forearm,
approximately two centimeters.'

Permitted action — the boundary link, and the one competitors miss: what may YOUR role do? A
volunteer reports it and stays with the patient. Cleaning it, dressing it, judging whether it's
serious — that belongs to licensed staff, and saying so out loud earns points: 'I'm not able to
treat this, but I'll get the person who can, right now.'

[PAUSE — Q: Why does 'what I OBSERVE' come before any explanation — why not name the condition
immediately?]

Because observation is what your role owns. 'I see a scraped area' is always safe and always true.
Jumping to conclusions — 'that's infected' — is diagnosis, and diagnosis belongs to someone else.

That's the pattern for any term: know it, observe with it, explain it plainly, report it precisely,
and act only inside your role. Your terminology deck feeds the written test. The bridge feeds the
performance. You need both, and CompeteReady drills both."

**Practice prompt:** "Bridge drill: take a term from your deck and write all five sentences —
observation, patient explanation, professional report, and your role's permitted action."
**Retake instruction:** "Check your patient sentence for the raw term, and your action sentence for
anything a volunteer can't do. Fix only the failing link."
**Editing notes:** the six-link chain must persist and light up per link; end card: educational
example, not medical guidance, not official HOSA content.

- **Objective:** the learner can name what fails in a jargon-first, over-promising interaction and
  what works in an acknowledge-first, bounded one.
- **Duration:** ~4.5 min. **Placement:** HOSA Branch B worked-example companion — **currently held.**
- **Framing:** presenter plays the assigned role; off-camera voice plays the patient. Identical framing
  both takes.

**Script (spoken):**

"One scenario, performed twice. I'm a clinic volunteer at the front desk. The patient checking in is
anxious, and asks: 'Who can actually see my medical information? I don't want my family or my job
finding out what I'm here for.'

Take one — watch for three failures:

(flat, recited) 'Under HIPAA, protected health information is restricted to covered entities per the
minimum necessary standard.'
(off-screen, smaller voice) 'I… don't know what that means. I just don't want my job finding out.'
'Don't worry, no one will ever see anything, I promise. You can go sit down.'

[PAUSE — Q: Name the three failures. One is about jargon, one about promises, one about the ending.]

Failure one: jargon at a frightened person — 'covered entities' made her MORE scared, not less.
Failure two: 'no one will ever see anything, I promise' — a volunteer cannot promise that; it's an
overpromise outside the role, and it's not even true.
Failure three: 'go sit down' — no check for understanding, no next step. She leaves unheard.

Take two — same person, same question:

(warm, eye contact) 'That's a really understandable thing to worry about — a lot of people ask this.
Can I ask — is there someone in particular you're concerned might find out? Your employer, or
family?'
(off-screen) 'My employer. I don't want work knowing I was here.'
'Okay. In plain terms: your medical information here is private. It's shared with the care team
looking after you, and it isn't given to your employer without your written permission. So coming in
today doesn't get reported to your job. Would it help if I brought over our nurse or privacy
officer? They can walk you through exactly how your information is protected.'
(off-screen) '…Yes. Thank you.'
'Of course. I'll get them now — and I'm glad you asked.'

[SCREEN: ACKNOWLEDGE → CLARIFY → RESPOND SAFELY → CHECK UNDERSTANDING → NEXT STEP]

Same volunteer, same knowledge. The second take acknowledged first, found the REAL worry with one
question, answered it in plain language, stayed inside the role — notice: accurate reassurance, no
absolute promises — and ended with a next step instead of a brush-off.

That framework on screen is the whole event in five moves. Every lesson in this course trains one of
them."

**Practice prompt:** "The patient's follow-up: 'What if someone I know works here and looks at my
file out of curiosity?' Write your response — honest, bounded, with a next step."
**Retake instruction:** "Check your response for the word 'never' or 'promise.' If it's there,
you've overpromised — rewrite with honest reassurance plus the right referral."
**Editing notes:** takes must be uncut; patient voice must sound genuinely worried, not theatrical;
end card disclaimers (not official; educational example, not privacy/legal advice).

## VIDEO 20 — Staying Within Your Assigned Role

- **Objective:** the learner can recognize scope-of-role violations and produce the bounded
  alternative for each.
- **Duration:** ~4 min. **Placement:** HOSA Branch B, lesson B3 (Your Assigned Role and Its Boundaries).
- **Framing:** medium shot; violation/alternative pairs shown as cards.

**Script (spoken):**

"The fastest way to lose points in any health-science scenario isn't ignorance — it's helpfulness
that oversteps. Today: the boundary rule, and why staying inside it is a skill judges score, not a
limitation to apologize for.

Your assigned role — volunteer, aide, tech — can only do what that role is really allowed to do.
Four violations, and the bounded move that replaces each:

[SCREEN: card pairs appear as spoken]

Violation one — diagnosing: 'That rash looks like an allergic reaction.'
Bounded move: 'I see the redness you're describing. Let me get the nurse to take a proper look.'
Observe and refer. Never conclude.

Violation two — promising outcomes: 'You'll be fine, this is nothing serious.'
Bounded move: 'I can't assess that myself, but the right person can, and I'll bring them now.'
Comfort comes from action, not from guarantees you can't make.

Violation three — medical advice: 'You could probably take an extra dose if it hurts.'
Bounded move: 'Medication questions need the pharmacist or the nurse — let me connect you right
away.' A volunteer NEVER advises on medication. Ever.

Violation four — information beyond your access: 'Let me look up your results for you.'
Bounded move: 'I don't have access to results, but I'll find out who can review them with you.'

[PAUSE — Q: All four violations came from trying to HELP. What do all four bounded moves have in
common?]

Every one observes honestly, refuses to guess, and ends with a referral to the right professional.
That's the pattern: I can't do X — and here's the person who can, right now. Said calmly, that
sentence is not weakness. On the rating sheet, it's competence.

And when you're not sure whether something is inside your role? Treat that uncertainty as the
answer: refer. The follow-up question that tempts you to overstep is frequently the whole point.

[SCREEN: What the competition uses · what CompeteReady's policy is — two different things]

Two last things, and they're deliberately separate.

**What the competition uses is set by your event's guideline and equipment list.** Some events provide
a live patient or an actor for the noninvasive parts. Invasive steps, medication administration, blood
draws, injections, and resuscitation use manikins, training arms, medication trainers, or whatever
simulation equipment your event specifies. Which applies to you is in your guideline — that is the only
place to look it up, and it changes between events.

**And this part is CompeteReady's policy, not a HOSA rule** — HOSA doesn't publish a supervision
requirement, so we're not going to pretend it does. Ours is: practise hands-on clinical skills with
appropriate supervision and the right equipment, with your instructor. We will never teach, score, or
simulate a clinical procedure here, and nothing you do in this app makes you clinically ready.

Know your lane. Own your lane. Hand off everything outside it — quickly, warmly, and without
apology."

**Practice prompt:** "Branch B gives you four tempting patient requests. Sort: inside or outside your
assigned role. For each 'outside,' write the bounded alternative."
**Retake instruction:** "Any bounded alternative missing a referral ('…and I'll get the nurse')? Add
the handoff — refusal without referral is only half the skill."
**Editing notes:** violation cards in amber, bounded cards in green, ALWAYS with text labels (never
color alone — accessibility rule); end card disclaimers.
