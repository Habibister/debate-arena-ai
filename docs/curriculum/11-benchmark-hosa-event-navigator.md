## What it is

> Here's the mistake that costs HOSA competitors more points than any other — and it happens before
> practice even begins: **preparing for the wrong kind of event.**
>
> HOSA is not one format. It's a family of very different competitions. Some events are written
> tests. Some are presentations you build in advance. Some are hands-on clinical skills performed
> for evaluators. Some are team events. Some are emergency-preparedness events. And some are
> scenario events — a professional interaction you perform in an assigned role.
>
> Flashcards are the right training for a terminology test and the wrong training for a patient
> interaction. A rehearsed presentation is the right training for a speaking event and useless for
> a skills check-off. So before anything else, this lesson teaches you to answer three questions:
> **What exactly is my event? What does it contain? What does its rating sheet reward?** Then — and
> only then — you train the parts that match.

> **Implementation status (2026-07-31).** The shipped Navigator does not display an official HOSA
> category: those names are not in the approved source record, and the product refuses to guess one. The
> "Official category" idea below therefore remains a benchmark for future work, gated on sourcing it.
> What ships today is a CompeteReady training grouping, labelled as such where the learner browses.

### Key ideas

> - **Official category** — HOSA groups its events into official categories, and the category names
>   come from the current guideline index — not from what people call them online. We'll show you the
>   real ones. Beginners routinely use category words without a working model of the taxonomy, which
>   is exactly how people end up preparing for the wrong kind of event.
> - **Components** — many events combine parts (for example, a written test AND a performance
>   round). Each component is trained differently.
> - **Round structure** — how many rounds your event has, what advances you, and **when you find
>   out**. This varies more than beginners expect.
> - **Prejudged or onsite** — some events are decided partly or wholly by material you submit weeks
>   in advance; others happen entirely at the conference. This changes your whole calendar.
> - **The rating sheet** — the official scoring document for your event. It is literally the list
>   of what earns points — which makes it your practice plan. **Read it before you choose, not after
>   you've committed.**
> - **This year's guideline** — events change: formats, question counts, and rating sheets get
>   revised. (Medical Terminology's own test length changed recently.) Last year's notes are a rumor;
>   the current guideline is the rule. **Check the date on the document itself** — not the web page it
>   sits on.
> - **Your association matters** — HOSA's guidelines are written for the International Leadership
>   Conference. **Chartered associations may run things differently**, and advancement is decided by
>   your association. If someone in another state tells you how competitive an event is, that's their
>   state, not yours.
> - **What an app can and can't train** — CompeteReady can train knowledge, decoding, communication,
>   and rating-sheet reading. **It cannot train your hands.** The physical clinical skill needs
>   in-person practice with your instructor — that's our policy, not a HOSA rule — and finishing a
>   communication branch here does not mean you're ready for the complete event. We will always tell
>   you which is which.

## Finding your event, step by step

> 1. **Get the exact name.** From your advisor or the official HOSA event list — not "something
>    medical," the exact event name.
> 2. **Open this year's guideline.** The current official document for that event. Confirm the date
>    on the document itself — not the page it's linked from. If the link is dead or the document
>    won't open, ask your advisor rather than substituting an older copy you found elsewhere.
> 3. **List the components.** Written test? Presentation? Skill performance? Interview? Portfolio?
>    Write down every part your event contains.
> 4. **Find the round structure.** How many rounds, what carries forward, what advances you — and
>    whether any part is **prejudged** from something you submit in advance. Note when results are
>    normally posted, so the wait doesn't blindside you.
> 5. **Read the rating sheet.** For each performance component, the sheet names the scored
>    behaviors, usually step by step with points attached, and often a threshold you must clear.
>    Underline the verbs — they are your skills list. **Do this before you commit to the event**, not
>    the week before the conference.
> 6. **Check your association.** Your chartered association may modify how the event runs, and it
>    decides advancement. Its site — or your advisor — is the authority for that, not another state's
>    experience.
> 7. **Split your training.** Match each component to the right practice: knowledge → question
>    banks and spaced review; communication inside a clinical skill → the communication branch;
>    interview → the interview branch; presentation → structure and delivery lessons; **hands-on
>    skill → your instructor's lab**, with the knowledge and communication layers here.
> 8. **Confirm in the Event Navigator.** Select your event in the app; it shows what we can train
>    for it today, what's optional enrichment, and what needs in-person practice — honestly
>    labeled, with sources where we have them. Where we don't have your event's official structure
>    yet, it will say so rather than guess.

### Test day and conference basics ⟨NEW — H7/HR-9⟩

> Some points are lost before anyone evaluates your skills. At the international level, expect: photo
> identification requirements with real penalties for missing it, rules about arriving late, a dress
> code with a bonus attached to meeting it — **with an exception for Academic Testing Center events**
> — equipment you may be expected to bring, and upload deadlines for anything prejudged. On test
> events, know the mechanics too: how the answer sheet works, what calculator is allowed, what
> reference material is supplied.
>
> **These are International Leadership Conference rules. Your association may differ — check.**

## One competitor, planned two ways

**Weak preparation** *(annotated)*

> "I'm doing a HOSA event this year. It's medical, so I'm grinding flashcards every night. My
> friend did a role-play at her competition, so I'm also practicing patient conversations. I'll
> find out the details when I get there."
>
> ▸ No exact event name — everything downstream is a guess.
> ▸ Training copied from a friend's DIFFERENT event.
> ▸ "Find out when I get there" means the rating sheet — the actual scoring list — was never read.
> This competitor may be working hard on skills their event doesn't score at all.

**Strong preparation** *(annotated)*

> "My event is [exact event name], from this year's official guideline. It has two components: a
> written test, and a timed skill performance with a rating sheet. So my plan is split: for the
> test, question banks with spaced review. For the performance, I printed the rating sheet,
> underlined every scored behavior, and I practice the hands-on steps with my instructor Tuesdays —
> the app drills me on the knowledge and on verbalizing the steps and reporting clearly, which the
> sheet also scores."
>
> ▸ Exact event, current-year source, components listed.
> ▸ The rating sheet — not a guess — is the practice plan.
> ▸ Training is split correctly: knowledge and communication here, psychomotor skill in supervised
> practice. Nothing wasted, nothing invented.

## Common mistakes

> 1. **Training for a different event than yours.** Copying a teammate's practice plan when their
>    event has different components. Fix: your event's guideline, this year, read first.
> 2. **Preparing a conversation for a test event (or flashcards for a scenario event).** Component
>    mismatch. Fix: list your event's components before choosing any practice.
> 3. **Never reading the rating sheet.** Practicing what feels productive instead of what's scored.
>    Fix: underline the sheet's verbs; practice those.
> 4. **Using last year's rules.** Formats and counts change between seasons. Fix: confirm the date
>    on the current document.
> 5. **Believing an app can teach hands-on clinical skill.** It can't — and any product that
>    pretends otherwise is training you badly. Fix: knowledge and communication here; hands-on
>    skill with your instructor.

## Practice — plan your own event

**Identify (deterministic, authored answers):**

> **Q1.** Your event's guideline says it includes a written test AND a timed performance with a
> rating sheet. What's the right training split?
> Choices: Flashcards only — tests matter most / Scenario conversations only / **Question banks +
> spaced review for the test; rating-sheet behaviors (with your instructor for hands-on parts) for
> the performance ✓** / Watch videos of past winners
> Explanation: "Each component gets its own matched practice. One-tool training loses whichever
> half you skipped."
>
> **Q2.** Your teammate's event has a patient role-play, and you've been practicing conversations
> with her. Your event is a knowledge test. What's true?
> Choices: The practice transfers — it's all HOSA / **You're training for her event, not yours —
> check your own guideline and re-plan ✓** / Conversations are always scored in HOSA / You should
> switch events
> Explanation: "HOSA events differ completely. Training must come from YOUR event's current
> guideline, not a teammate's format."
>
> **Q3.** Where do you find exactly what your performance is scored on?
> Choices: Reddit threads from past competitors / A teammate's memory / **Your event's official
> rating sheet in the current guideline ✓** / This app's general scenario lessons
> Explanation: "The rating sheet is the scoring instrument itself. Everything else — including our
> lessons — is training toward it, not a substitute for reading it."
>
> **Q4.** Your event includes a hands-on clinical skill. What can CompeteReady honestly train?
> Choices: The full skill, virtually / Nothing — skip the app / **The knowledge behind it, plus
> verbalizing steps, reporting, and communication — while the psychomotor skill itself needs
> supervised in-person practice ✓** / A simulation that certifies you're ready
> Explanation: "We train the layers an app can truly train, and we tell you plainly which layer
> needs your instructor. No fake readiness."

**Write (the learner's own words):**

> "Write your own event plan, in 3–5 sentences: your event's exact name (or 'I need to confirm it
> with my advisor' — that's a legitimate first step), the components it contains, and which kind of
> practice each component gets."
> Placeholder: "My event is… It has these components… For each one, my practice is…"

**Feedback rubric (sent as `goals`):**

> 1. Names the exact event — or honestly states that confirming it with the advisor is step one
> 2. Lists the event's components rather than assuming one format
> 3. Matches each component to the right kind of practice (knowledge / scenario / presentation /
>    supervised hands-on)
> 4. Uses the current-year guideline or rating sheet as the source of truth — not a teammate,
>    a memory, or a guess

**Follow-up (authored, in the advisor's voice):**

> **Advisor:** "Good plan. One thing's missing — how will you know you're improving on the
> performance part, not just the test part?"
> Note: "Answer with something observable from the rating sheet — a scored behavior you can
> practice and check — not 'I'll feel more confident.'"

**Immediate redo (required):** same mechanics as all benchmark lessons — the plan reopens beside
the feedback; the block completes on the revised version. Feedback failures show an honest
"coach unavailable — retry" state, never canned text.

**Transfer:**

> "A friend tells you: 'My HOSA event is in March, so I'm just going to memorize terminology every
> day until then.' You don't know their event. What's the FIRST question you'd ask them, and why?
> (One sentence each.)"
> *(Model answer shown after submission: "What exactly is your event, and what does its current
> guideline say it contains? — because until the components are known, no practice plan can be
> right.")*
