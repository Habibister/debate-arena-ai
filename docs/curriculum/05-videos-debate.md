# 05 — Video Curriculum: Debate (videos 1–8, full scripts)

Status: **DRAFT V2** — revised per the approved Final Research Synthesis + Correction Addendum.
Changes in this revision: the coin toss and the crossfire-is-Q&A correction (video 1); final-focus
new-argument language treated as a **dated ballot instruction, not a universal rule** (video 1);
flexible collapse and "even if" as **one** comparative construction, not a universal norm (video 1);
flowing enrichment — preflow, column-per-speech, answers beside the argument, drops-as-gaps, two-color,
optional author-name indexing (video 5).

**Constraint carried into every script: no numeric speaking-rate target is stated anywhere.** Rate,
cadence, and articulation are taught diagnostically (doc 01 L48), never as a number.

All scripts are original. Presenter = the user (on camera). Conventions used in every script:

- **[SCREEN: …]** = on-screen text/graphic at that moment.
- **[PAUSE — Q: …]** = pause point; the player shows the learner question; learner answers before
  resuming.
- **Caption text** = the spoken script verbatim (closed captions), plus bracketed sound labels where
  relevant. No separate caption copy is written unless noted.
- **Framing default:** medium shot, eye-level, presenter centered, clean background, key light from
  the front-left; cut-ins noted per video. Record horizontal 16:9; keep safe area for captions.
- **Retake instruction** = the on-screen instruction that tells the LEARNER to re-record/redo their
  own attempt (deliberate-practice step), shown at the end.

## VIDEO 1 — What a Debate Round Looks Like

- **Objective:** the learner can name the speeches of a Public Forum round in order and say what job
  each speech does.
- **Duration:** ~4 min. **Placement:** Debate Lesson 0.
- **Framing:** medium shot; cut to a full-screen round-structure graphic during the walkthrough.

**Script (spoken):**

"If you've never seen a debate round, here's the whole thing in four minutes — so when you walk into
your first one, nothing surprises you.

A Public Forum round is two teams of two people, arguing opposite sides of one resolution — one
topic. Nobody wins by shouting. You win by making arguments a judge can compare, and comparing them
better than the other team.

[SCREEN: full PF round order, per the current sourced registry structure]

Before any of it starts, there's a coin toss. The team that wins it chooses one of two things — which
side they'll argue, or whether they'll speak first or last. The team that loses the toss gets whichever
choice is left. Small moment, real strategic decision.

Here's the shape of the round. Both teams give a constructive — that's a prepared speech laying out
their case. Then the first speakers question each other directly. That's crossfire — and this matters:
crossfire is question-and-answer time, not a speech the judge scores on its own. It's where you set up
admissions you'll use later.

Then rebuttals: each team attacks the other's case. Directly. Line by line. If your opponent said
'later school starts improve grades,' your rebuttal says why that's wrong, or why it doesn't matter.

[PAUSE — Q: The other team ignored your best argument completely. Is that good or bad for you? Why?]

If they ignored it — that's called a drop, and it's great for you. An unanswered argument usually
counts as true for the rest of the round.

Then summaries: each team picks its winning arguments — you can't keep everything — and starts
comparing. One useful way to do that is the 'even if' move: 'even if they're right about cost, lives
outweigh cost.' That's one construction that works; it isn't the only one, and you'll hear strong
debaters compare without ever using the words magnitude, probability, or timeframe.

Grand crossfire: all four debaters, same rules as before. And final focus: two minutes to hand the
judge your decision — narrow the round down to the fewest issues that still tell a complete story.
That might be one issue. It might be two or three. What matters is that nothing you're still standing
on goes unexplained.

[SCREEN: Ballot instructions often say to avoid new arguments in the final focus — check your
tournament's current ballot]

One note on new arguments in the final focus: official ballots have carried instructions about this,
but the wording is dated and circuits vary. Treat it as a ballot instruction to check, not a universal
rule you can assume.

The judge decides on what was argued and answered — not on which side they personally like. That's
the whole game: make arguments, answer theirs, and by the end, hand the judge the reason to vote for
you.

[SCREEN: Make arguments → Answer theirs → Weigh → Hand the judge the decision]

One more thing: the exact times are in the official rules, and they're shown next to every practice
round in CompeteReady — you never have to memorize them today."

**Weak demonstration (inside video):** none — structural video.
**Strong demonstration:** the graphic walkthrough itself.
**Practice prompt (end card):** "Open Lesson 0 and put the speeches in order from memory. Then answer:
which speech is the first one allowed to attack the other side's case?"
**Retake instruction:** "Got any wrong? Watch the graphic section again at 2x, then retry the
ordering before moving on."
**Editing notes:** cut tight; the round graphic should build piece by piece, not appear whole;
highlight the current speech while it's discussed.

## VIDEO 2 — Defending a Side You Disagree With

- **Objective:** the learner can explain why arguing an assigned side is a professional role, and can
  steelman a position they personally reject without lying.
- **Duration:** ~3.5 min. **Placement:** Debate Lesson 6 (Defending a Side You Do Not Personally Support).
- **Framing:** medium shot; slow push-in during the steelman demo.

**Script (spoken):**

"Sooner or later you'll be assigned a side you personally disagree with. Every debater faces this,
and it feels weird the first time. Let's fix that today.

First: arguing a side is not lying. A defense lawyer isn't claiming their client is innocent as a
personal belief — they're doing a job the system needs: making sure the strongest honest case gets
heard. Debate is the same. The judge is not scoring your private opinions. They're scoring whether
you can build and defend a case.

[SCREEN: PERSONAL BELIEF ≠ ASSIGNED ADVOCACY]

Here's what you are NOT allowed to do — ever: invent evidence, misquote sources, misrepresent your
opponent, or pretend the position is your personal confession. None of that is advocacy. That's just
lying, and good judges smell it.

Here's what you ARE doing. Watch the difference.

Weak version — this is me arguing a side I dislike, badly:

'So, um, we're supposed to argue that homework should be banned. I guess some people think it's
stressful… I don't know, that's our case.'

Hear it? I'm apologizing for my own side. The judge has nothing to vote for.

[PAUSE — Q: What exactly made that weak — the argument, or the attitude? Say it out loud.]

Now the steelman. Same side, done right:

'Reasonable people support this because the evidence on homework in early grades is genuinely mixed,
because take-home work multiplies inequality — some students have quiet rooms and tutors, some have
jobs and siblings to watch — and because schools that replaced homework with in-class practice did
not see performance drop. That's the case I'm defending today.'

I didn't lie once. I found the real reasons real people hold this position, and I committed to them.

[SCREEN: SEPARATE BELIEF FROM ROLE → UNDERSTAND → STEELMAN → SUPPORT ACCURATELY → ANTICIPATE
OBJECTIONS → ADVOCATE WITH COMMITMENT]

That six-step loop is the whole skill. And here's the secret nobody tells beginners: arguing the side
you disagree with makes you better, because you finally see your own side's weaknesses from the
inside."

**Practice prompt:** "Pick an opinion you actually hold. Now write three honest sentences a
reasonable person on the OTHER side would say. No strawmen."
**Retake instruction:** "Read your three sentences out loud, committed, like the strong demo. If you
hear apology in your voice, record it again."
**Editing notes:** visually separate the weak demo (small desaturated frame) from the strong demo
(full frame); do not make the weak demo comic — it must be recognizably normal beginner behavior.

## VIDEO 3 — Ethos, Pathos, and Logos Using One Motion

- **Objective:** the learner can identify logos, pathos, and ethos in an argument and explain why an
  argument missing one of them fails.
- **Duration:** ~4.5 min. **Placement:** Debate Lesson 17 (pairs with the benchmark lesson, doc 09).
- **Framing:** medium shot; three labeled demo segments; final combined delivery framed slightly
  wider.

**Script (spoken):**

"One motion, four performances. The motion: schools should begin later in the morning. Watch what
happens when an argument has only logic, only emotion, or only confidence — and then what happens
when it has all three.

Performance one. Logic alone — logos with nothing else:

'Adolescent circadian rhythms shift approximately two hours during puberty. A later start time
increases average sleep duration, which correlates with improved academic performance. Therefore
schools should begin later.'

[PAUSE — Q: Every sentence was accurate. Why doesn't it move you?]

It's true — and it's dead. Nobody in that argument is a person. Nothing bad is happening to anyone.
Accurate premises with no human meaning give the judge no reason to care.

Performance two. Emotion alone — pathos with no proof:

'Every morning, exhausted kids drag themselves onto dark buses. They're suffering! How can we do
this to children? It's cruel, and everyone knows it.'

Feel the problem? It's loud, but it proves nothing. The moment an opponent asks 'what's your
evidence?', it collapses. Emotion without proof is just noise the judge can't vote on.

Performance three. Confidence alone — fake ethos:

'Look, this is obvious. Every expert agrees with me. There is literally no argument on the other
side. This debate is already over.'

That's not credibility, that's bluffing. Real ethos isn't claiming authority — it's behaving like
someone worth believing: being accurate, being fair to the other side, staying calm.

Now all three together.

'Adolescent sleep cycles genuinely shift in the teenage years — that's the biology, and it's not
controversial. Here's what it means in a hallway: a fifteen-year-old with a 7 a.m. start is sitting
in first period on six hours of sleep, learning nothing, every single day. Yes — a later start
complicates buses and sports schedules; I won't pretend it doesn't. But districts that made this
change saw attendance and performance improve. Weigh a scheduling problem against a learning
problem, and learning should win.'

[SCREEN: LOGOS = the mechanism · PATHOS = the human stake · ETHOS = earned trust]

Notice what built the ethos there: I conceded a real tradeoff. Honesty is a persuasion technique —
the only one that also happens to be right."

**Practice prompt:** "Take the argument you built in Module 2. Mark one sentence carrying logos, add
one sentence of honest human stake, and add one honest concession. Deliver all three."
**Retake instruction:** "Record your delivery. If your concession sounds like surrender instead of
confidence, do a second take — concede in a steady voice, then pivot with 'but'."
**Editing notes:** label each performance with a persistent corner tag (LOGOS ONLY / PATHOS ONLY /
FAKE ETHOS / ALL THREE); the combined performance gets no jump cuts — one take, so pacing is real.

## VIDEO 4 — Weak Versus Strong Debate Delivery

- **Objective:** the learner can name five concrete delivery behaviors that change how the same text
  lands, and self-diagnose them on video.
- **Duration:** ~3.5 min. **Placement:** Debate Lesson 49 (Debate as Performance).
- **Framing:** full/medium shot (body language must be visible); identical framing for both demos so
  delivery is the only variable.

**Script (spoken):**

"I'm going to give the same thirty-second speech twice. Same words. Watch what changes.

Take one:

(rushed, flat, eyes down, trailing upward at line ends, small apologetic posture)
'Our first argument is that later school starts improve learning? Because students would get more
sleep? And sleep is important for memory… so, yeah, that's why we think schools should start later.'

Take two:

(measured pace, eyes up, a real pause after the first sentence, downward-committed endings,
emphasis on 'every single day')
'Our first argument: later starts improve learning. (pause) Students learn more when they sleep
more — that's memory science, and it plays out in first period every single day. That's why schools
should start later.'

[PAUSE — Q: Same words. Name two specific things that changed.]

Here's the checklist of what you just saw.

[SCREEN: 1. Pace — slow enough to follow · 2. Endings — statements end DOWN, not up ·
3. Eyes — on the judge, notes as reference · 4. Pauses — after the claims that matter ·
5. Commitment — sound like you believe your own case]

Weak delivery isn't a personality flaw — it's a set of habits: rushing, uptalk, reading, hiding.
Strong delivery is also just habits: pace, downward endings, eye contact, purposeful pauses, and
committed tone. Habits are trainable. That's the whole point of the delivery module.

One warning: strong delivery is not exaggeration. You're not performing anger or fake passion.
You're performing certainty about an honest case — controlled, clear, and purposeful."

**Practice prompt:** "Record yourself delivering any 30-second argument twice: once however it comes
out, once applying the five checklist items."
**Retake instruction:** "Play your first recording with the checklist visible. Mark which of the
five you missed. Re-record attacking ONE of them — not all five at once."
**Editing notes:** the two takes must be uncut singles; show the checklist as a persistent sidebar
during the second take replay.

## VIDEO 5 — Building and Flowing a Rebuttal

- **Objective:** the learner can flow an opponent's argument in two columns and deliver a four-step
  rebuttal against it.
- **Duration:** ~4.5 min. **Placement:** Debate Lessons 30–31 (Flowing deep; Four-Step Refutation).
- **Framing:** medium shot alternating with overhead/insert shot of a real flow sheet being written.

**Script (spoken):**

"Rebuttal is where beginners freeze — 'they made an argument, and my mind went blank.' The fix is a
system, and it has two halves: writing it down, and answering in four steps.

First, the flow. When your opponent speaks, you're not just listening — you're writing their
argument's skeleton in a column.

[INSERT: overhead of flow sheet being written live]

Suppose they argue: 'Schools shouldn't start later, because buses would cost millions, and that
money comes from classrooms.' On my sheet: 'buses $$ → cuts classrooms.' Six words. I don't write
sentences — I write hooks I can answer.

Next to it, in the second column, I jot my answer as it occurs to me: 'other districts phased buses
— no cuts.' That's flowing: their skeleton, my response, side by side.

[PAUSE — Q: Why write their argument down instead of trusting memory?]

Because in the next speech, the arguments you don't answer are the ones that count against you —
and under pressure, memory drops things. The flow is your insurance against drops.

[SCREEN: Preflow before the round · one column per speech · answers beside the argument · gaps = drops]

Four habits make a flow usable instead of decorative. Preflow your own case before the round starts,
so you're only writing *their* material once the round begins. Give every speech its own column, so
you can read an argument left to right across the whole round. Write your answer physically beside the
argument it answers, never at the bottom. And let the gaps do work for you — an empty space next to an
argument is a dropped argument, visible at a glance.

Two colors help: one for what they said, one for what you'll say. And write taglines, not evidence —
the label of the argument, not the card behind it. If an evidence dispute ends up centring on one
particular source, some debaters find it useful to note that author's name as an index. That's an
option, not a requirement; the argument label and your actual response matter far more than
remembering whose study it was.

Now the four steps. Watch them land on the bus argument:

[SCREEN: THEY SAY → BUT → BECAUSE → THEREFORE]

'They say later starts are too expensive because of busing. (THEY SAY — name it fairly.)
But that cost is a one-time transition, not a permanent cut. (BUT — your direct answer.)
Because districts that made this change phased bus routes over two years and reported no classroom
cuts — the costs they're describing didn't happen. (BECAUSE — the reason your answer is true.)
Therefore cost is not a reason to keep failing schedules — and learning still outweighs a logistics
problem. (THEREFORE — why the round now leans your way.)'

That's a complete rebuttal: fair summary, direct clash, a warrant, and a weighing move. No step
skipped. The most common beginner miss is stopping after BUT — an answer with no BECAUSE is just a
counter-claim, and the judge has no reason to prefer it."

**Practice prompt:** "In the app, flow the 40-second authored speech in Lesson 29, then deliver a
four-step rebuttal against its main argument. The rubric checks all four steps."
**Retake instruction:** "If your rebuttal skipped BECAUSE, don't start over — keep your first two
steps and re-record from BECAUSE."
**Editing notes:** the flow insert must show real handwriting speed (slightly sped up is fine, label
it); keep the four-step graphic on screen during the entire demo rebuttal.

## VIDEO 6 — Thinking on Your Feet

- **Objective:** the learner can apply the listen→identify→acknowledge→answer→return loop to a
  surprise question without freezing.
- **Duration:** ~4 min. **Placement:** Debate Lesson 40 (Thinking on Your Feet).
- **Framing:** two-position setup if possible (presenter answers questions read by an off-camera
  voice); otherwise single camera with the question as on-screen text.

**Script (spoken):**

"Everyone freezes in their first crossfire. The difference between beginners and experienced
debaters isn't that experienced people are faster — it's that they have somewhere to go when their
mind blanks. Here's the somewhere.

[SCREEN: LISTEN → IDENTIFY THE EXACT DISAGREEMENT → ACKNOWLEDGE ANY VALID POINT → ANSWER →
RETURN TO THE DECISIVE ISSUE]

Watch it work. Surprise question:

(off-screen) 'Your whole case assumes students would actually sleep more. Won't they just stay up
later?'

Freeze response — what panic looks like:

'Um… I mean… no, they would sleep, because… sleep is important…'

Now the loop, same question:

(beat of silence — that's LISTEN, and a second of silence is allowed)
'The exact disagreement is whether extra morning time becomes sleep or becomes screen time. (IDENTIFY)
And you're right that some students will stay up later — that's real. (ACKNOWLEDGE — it costs me
nothing and buys credibility.)
But the studies on districts that shifted start times measured actual sleep, not intentions — and
average sleep went up. (ANSWER, with a warrant.)
So the question for this round is still the one we started with: does more measured sleep improve
learning? It does. (RETURN.)'

[PAUSE — Q: What did the one-second pause before answering signal — weakness or control?]

Control. A calm second of thought reads as strength. Panic-filling the silence with 'um' reads as
weakness. You're allowed to think.

Two emergency tools for when you're truly stuck. The holding move: 'The direct answer is—' — saying
it forces your brain to produce a direct answer. And the honest concession: 'I'll concede that
specific point — and it doesn't decide the round, because…' Conceding a small true thing is not
losing; it's steering."

**Practice prompt:** "Open the surprise-question drill. You get one question, ten seconds of think
time, and sixty seconds to answer using all five steps."
**Retake instruction:** "Whatever happened, redo the SAME question immediately — the second attempt
is where the loop becomes yours."
**Editing notes:** keep the freeze demo short (under 8 seconds) — it should be uncomfortable, not
mocking; put the five-step graphic back on screen during the loop demo, highlighting each step as it
happens.

## VIDEO 7 — Logical Fallacy Versus Weak Argument

- **Objective:** the learner can distinguish a fallacy (broken inference) from a merely weak
  argument, and respond by explaining the broken link instead of naming the fallacy.
- **Duration:** ~4 min. **Placement:** Debate Lesson 25 (What a Logical Fallacy Actually Is).
- **Framing:** medium shot; split-screen cards for the two example arguments.

**Script (spoken):**

"Beginners learn ten fallacy names and start yelling them like spells. 'That's a strawman!' — and
then they lose the round anyway. Today: what a fallacy actually is, and what to do instead of naming
it.

A fallacy is a broken inference — the conclusion does not follow from the premises, even if every
premise is true. A weak argument is different: the logic connects, but the support is thin.

[SCREEN: FALLACY = conclusion doesn't follow · WEAK = follows, but thinly supported]

Two arguments about the same topic. First:

'My cousin's school moved to a later start and her grades went up. So later starts raise grades —
proven.'

Second:

'One study of 30 schools found later starts modestly improved grades. So later starts will help
here.'

[PAUSE — Q: One is a fallacy, one is just weak. Which is which?]

The first is the fallacy — a hasty generalization. One cousin cannot carry a conclusion about all
schools; the link between premise and conclusion is broken. The second is weak but valid — the
inference connects; you'd attack it by asking whether 30 schools generalize, whether the effect was
lasting. Different problems, different responses.

Now — here's the move that actually scores. Never just name it. Do this instead:

[SCREEN: IDENTIFY THE CLAIM → FIND THE UNSUPPORTED LINK → EXPLAIN WHY IT DOESN'T FOLLOW →
RESPOND DIRECTLY]

Against the cousin argument: 'Their claim is that later starts raise grades. Their entire support is
one student. One improving student can't tell us anything about a policy for millions — her grades
could have risen for a dozen reasons. Until they offer evidence at the scale of schools, this
argument can't support their conclusion.'

No fallacy name required. I explained the broken link, and the judge — who may not know the Latin
names either — followed every word.

Last step, and it's the one that makes you better instead of just louder: after every round, audit
your OWN case for the same broken links. Fallacy training is a mirror, not a weapon."

**Practice prompt:** "Lesson 19 gives you four arguments. Sort them: fallacy or weak-but-valid. For
one fallacy, write the explanation of the broken link — without using the fallacy's name."
**Retake instruction:** "If you used the fallacy's name in your explanation, rewrite it banned-word
style: explain the break in plain English only."
**Editing notes:** the two example arguments should stay visible as side-by-side cards during the
sorting question; the responding demo is one uncut take.

## VIDEO 8 — Feedback and Immediate Speech Redo

- **Objective:** the learner adopts the feedback→immediate-redo loop as their default practice
  habit, and can run one full cycle on a 60-second speech.
- **Duration:** ~3.5 min. **Placement:** Debate Practice Lab intro (and linked from Lesson 56, Video Self-Review).
- **Framing:** medium shot; includes a real redo demonstrated on camera.

**Script (spoken):**

"Here's the highest-value habit in this entire program, and almost nobody does it: when you get
feedback, redo the speech immediately. Not tomorrow. Now.

Why? Because feedback you don't act on evaporates. In every skill with real coaching — music,
sport, surgery — the loop is the same: attempt, specific feedback, immediate corrected attempt.
The correction is the practice. Reading feedback is not practice.

Watch one full cycle, for real. My first attempt — sixty-second version:

'Later school starts improve learning. Studies show students get more sleep, and sleep matters for
memory. Also it would help with attendance. So schools should start later.'

Honest feedback on that attempt — the same kind the app gives you:

[SCREEN: Worked: clear claim up front. · Improve: 'studies show' was never explained — which
students, what happened? · The attendance point appeared and vanished with no reason attached.]

Now the redo — same speech, sixty seconds later, fixing exactly those two things:

'Later school starts improve learning. When districts shifted start times, measured student sleep
went up — and more sleep means better memory consolidation, which showed up as higher first-period
grades. It also cut morning absences, because the students most likely to miss an early bus actually
made it to school. That's two mechanisms, one conclusion: schools should start later.'

[PAUSE — Q: The redo took one minute. What specifically got fixed between takes?]

That one minute is worth more than an hour of new attempts, because it practiced the correction
while the mistake was still warm.

In CompeteReady, every practice block ends with a redo box — your first answer stays on screen next
to the feedback, and you revise it before moving on. Don't skip it. The redo IS the rep."

**Practice prompt:** "Record any 60-second argument. Get feedback in the Practice Lab. Re-record
within two minutes, fixing only what the feedback named."
**Retake instruction:** "This entire video is the retake instruction. One redo, immediately, every
time."
**Editing notes:** show real elapsed-time stamps on the two takes ('Take 1 — 0:00', 'Take 2 —
1:40') to prove the immediacy; the feedback card must look like the app's real feedback UI (Worked /
Improve / Example).
