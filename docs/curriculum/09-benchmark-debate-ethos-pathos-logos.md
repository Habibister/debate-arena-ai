## Section 1 — What it is

> Three speeches can contain the same facts and land completely differently. The difference has had
> names for 2,300 years: logos, pathos, and ethos — the three channels through which any argument
> reaches a judge.
>
> **Logos** is the logic channel: your reasoning, your evidence, your causal mechanism — why the
> claim is actually true.
>
> **Pathos** is the human channel: why anyone should care. Not manufactured tears — real stakes,
> made vivid enough to feel.
>
> **Ethos** is the trust channel: whether YOU seem worth believing. Here's the part beginners get
> wrong: ethos is not confidence, credentials, or volume. It is earned inside the round — by being
> accurate, being fair to the other side, and staying calm.
>
> A judge is a human being. Logic tells them they *can* vote for you. Pathos tells them it
> *matters*. Ethos tells them they can *trust* what you said. Miss any channel and the other two
> weaken. This lesson shows you all three on one motion, then has you build them into your own case.

### Key ideas

> - **Logos** — the argument's engine: claim, warrant, evidence, mechanism. (You built this in
>   Module 2.)
> - **Pathos** — the argument's stakes: the honest human consequence, made concrete.
> - **Ethos** — the argument's credibility: accuracy, fairness, calm delivery, and honest
>   acknowledgment of tradeoffs.
> - **The trap** — each channel alone fails: logic without meaning is ignorable; emotion without
>   proof is noise; confidence without credibility is bluffing.

## Section 2 — One motion, four performances

> The motion, for all four performances: **"Schools should begin later in the morning."**
> Read the annotations — that's where the skill is.

**Performance 1 — Logos alone** *(labeled: LOGIC WITHOUT HUMAN SIGNIFICANCE)*

> "Adolescent circadian rhythms shift approximately two hours during puberty. A later start time
> increases average sleep duration. Increased sleep duration is associated with improved memory
> consolidation and academic performance. Therefore schools should begin later."
>
> ▸ Every sentence is accurate, and the chain connects. But no person appears anywhere in it.
> Nothing bad is happening to anyone. A judge can follow this argument perfectly — and feel no
> reason to prefer it over the other team's equally tidy chain.

**Performance 2 — Pathos alone** *(labeled: EMOTION WITHOUT PROOF)*

> "Every morning, exhausted kids drag themselves onto dark buses. They're suffering! Childhood is
> being stolen by an alarm clock. How can we keep doing this to children? Everyone knows it's
> cruel."
>
> ▸ Vivid — and empty. It asserts suffering without evidence and cruelty without mechanism. One
> opposing question — "what's your evidence sleep would actually increase?" — and there is nothing
> behind the feeling. The judge may nod along and still can't vote on it.

**Performance 3 — Fake ethos** *(labeled: CONFIDENCE WITHOUT CREDIBILITY)*

> "Look, this is obvious. Every expert agrees with us. There is literally no serious argument on
> the other side. This debate is honestly already over."
>
> ▸ This performs authority instead of earning it. "Every expert agrees" is unverifiable
> hand-waving; "no serious argument" disrespects a side that plainly has arguments (costs, buses,
> sports). Judges read this as bluffing — and once they distrust one sentence, they discount all of
> yours. That's the ethos channel running in reverse.

**Performance 4 — All three channels** *(labeled: THE COMPLETE ARGUMENT)*

> "Adolescent sleep cycles genuinely shift in the teenage years — that's well-established biology,
> not a debating point. *(▸ Logos: the mechanism, stated calmly as fact, not shouted.)*
> Here's what it means in a hallway: a fifteen-year-old with a 7 a.m. start is sitting in first
> period on six hours of sleep, learning nothing, every single day. *(▸ Pathos: the same fact,
> placed inside one concrete student's morning — stakes without exaggeration.)*
> Now — a later start genuinely complicates bus routes and after-school sports; I won't pretend it
> doesn't. *(▸ Ethos: an honest concession. Admitting a real tradeoff costs one sentence and buys
> credibility for every other sentence.)*
> But districts that made this change saw attendance and performance improve — the transition costs
> were temporary; the learning gains weren't. *(▸ Logos again: evidence answering the concession.)*
> So weigh it: a scheduling problem against a learning problem. Scheduling problems get solved with
> planning. Learning problems get solved with this motion." *(▸ The close weighs — and its calm,
> fair tone IS the ethos channel, running through delivery rather than any single sentence.)*

## Section 3 — Compare

> Put performances 1–3 next to performance 4 and notice: version 4 contains **the same logical
> chain as version 1** — it added a human being, a concession, and a comparison. Pathos didn't
> replace the logic; it gave the logic a reason to matter. Ethos wasn't a claim ("trust me"); it
> was behavior (accuracy + fairness + calm). That's the recipe, and it's repeatable.

## Section 4 — Common mistakes

> 1. **Treating pathos as crying.** Pathos is honest stakes made concrete — one real student's
>    6-hour night beats ten exclamation points. Fix: replace intensity words with specifics.
> 2. **Claiming ethos instead of earning it.** "Trust me," "obviously," "every expert agrees" are
>    anti-credibility. Fix: concede one true thing and be precise; credibility follows.
> 3. **Saving all pathos for a tearful ending.** Stakes belong next to the logic they animate, not
>    quarantined in a dramatic close. Fix: pair each mechanism with its human meaning.
> 4. **Letting emotion outrun proof.** If your stakes are bigger than your evidence, the gap reads
>    as manipulation. Fix: scale the feeling to what you actually showed.
> 5. **Confusing calm with flat.** Ethos-carrying delivery is controlled and committed, not
>    monotone. (Module 7 trains this.)

## Section 5 — Practice (guided; no scores recorded)

**Identify (deterministic, authored answers):**

> **Q1.** "A later start means a sophomore who works evenings finally gets real sleep instead of
> choosing between her shift and her first-period grade." — Which channel is this sentence mainly
> working?
> Choices: Logos / **Pathos ✓** / Ethos / None
> Explanation: "It's the human stakes of the policy, made concrete through one student. (It leans
> on logos from elsewhere — channels cooperate.)"
>
> **Q2.** Which sentence EARNS ethos rather than claiming it?
> Choices: "Trust me, I've researched this for months." / "Honestly, the other side has no real
> case." / **"The other side is right that buses are a real cost — and here's why it's worth it." ✓**
> / "Every expert agrees with our position."
> Explanation: "Fair acknowledgment of the other side's true point, followed by an answer, is how
> credibility is built inside a round."
>
> **Q3.** Your argument has strong evidence but the judge looks unmoved. Which channel most likely
> needs work?
> Choices: Logos / **Pathos ✓** / Ethos / Evidence quantity
> Explanation: "'Unmoved' is the pathos channel's failure signature: they can follow it but don't
> feel why it matters."
>
> **Q4.** What made Performance 3 fail?
> Choices: Its logic was wrong / It was too emotional / **It performed authority it hadn't earned
> and dismissed a side that had real arguments ✓** / It was too short
> Explanation: "Fake ethos: unverifiable authority claims plus unfairness to the other side make a
> judge discount everything else you say."

**Write (the learner's own words):**

> "Take this bare logos argument: *'Standardized-test retakes improve final scores, so schools
> should allow retakes.'* Rewrite it as a complete three-channel argument: keep the logic, add one
> sentence of honest concrete stakes (pathos), and one honest concession or precision move (ethos)."
> Placeholder: "The logic stays… Here's what it means for one student… And I'll grant that…"

**Feedback rubric (sent as `goals`; feedback quotes the learner's words):**

> 1. Keeps a clear logical claim with a warrant (logos intact)
> 2. Adds concrete, honest human stakes — a specific person or situation, not intensity words
> 3. Earns credibility: one honest concession or precise, fair statement (no "obviously"/"every
>    expert agrees")
> 4. The three channels support ONE argument (no bolted-on tearful ending)

**Immediate redo (required):**

> "Here's your draft next to the feedback. Revise it now — fix only what the feedback named, keep
> what worked. The redo is the rep."

**Judge-adaptation follow-up (in-character, authored):**

> **Skeptical judge:** "You've told me a story about one student. Stories aren't data. Why should
> this move my ballot?"
> Note: "Defend the pairing — explain what the concrete case does that the data alone doesn't, and
> point back to your evidence. Don't abandon the pathos; justify it."

**Transfer (new mini-scenario, same skill):**

> "New motion, no examples this time: *'Phones should be banned during the school day.'* Pick either
> side. Write a three-channel mini-argument (3–5 sentences). The rubric is the same four lines."
