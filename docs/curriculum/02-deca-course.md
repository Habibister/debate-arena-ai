### Module 1 — Reading the Situation
*Outcome: can decode a prompt and plan within the official prep window. Prerequisite: Module 0.
Applies to: role-play families. Classification: shared foundation.*

- **1. Understanding Your Role and the Judge's Role** — who you are, who the judge is playing, and why
  every sentence is addressed to that character. ⟨REVISED — B-6⟩ Adds the **judge-reality caveat**:
  judges are frequently volunteers, sometimes first-time, sometimes distracted. The
  "business executive" framing is a *model*, not a guarantee — build a response that survives a judge
  who is not tracking closely.
- **2. Reading and Decoding the Scenario** — the 5-extract method (my role / judge's role / problem /
  PIs / constraints). ⟨REVISED⟩ **Practice emphasises decoding under time pressure**, which is the
  reported bottleneck — not comprehension difficulty. Guided decode → timed decode → redo.
- **3. Understanding Performance Indicators** — ⟨D6⟩ PIs are **behaviors to demonstrate, never to
  recite**; judge instructions score only the listed PIs, and **PIs are not announced in advance.**
  Teaches the PI method: PI → PLAIN MEANING → SCENARIO REQUIREMENT → IN-CHARACTER ACTION →
  JUDGE-FACING EXPLANATION → MEASUREMENT.
  ⟨B-2 — UNRESOLVED, taught as a judgment call⟩ **Whether to name a PI's title aloud or weave it into
  the conversation is genuinely contested.** Teach **both options with their tradeoffs**; never present
  one as the answer. Official rule is *demonstrate* and is **silent on style**; an official page
  describing DECA's own sample presentations notes there is not a single right or wrong way to present
  — general support for avoiding one rigid script, and **not** evidence that judges treat the two
  styles equally. **Advisor/judge validation required.**
  ⟨B-7 — THIN EVIDENCE, labeled⟩ **Behavior-type PIs** ("Demonstrate…") get their own short drill.
  Evidence base is 2 discussions, below saturation — labeled as thin.
- **4. Using Preparation Time** — the 10 sourced minutes for Individual Series. ⟨REVISED — B-1⟩ Adds a
  **minute-by-minute prep budget**, a **hard note-length constraint (cue words, roughly three words
  per PI — not sentences)**, and a **filled example prep page.** Note bloat, not comprehension, is the
  reported failure mode. ⟨D19⟩ Materials are event-provided and prep notes are usable in the role-play
  — per the current official rules.

## DECA Preparation Sheet (taught in L4, reused everywhere)

One consistent layout, drilled until automatic. **Cue words only — not sentences.**

```
TOP:    My role | Judge's role | Main problem (one sentence)
LEFT:   Performance Indicators (as actions to demonstrate) — ~3-word cues each
CENTER: Recommendation 1 → business reason → implementation → measurement
RIGHT:  Recommendation 2 → business reason → implementation → measurement
BOTTOM: Opening line | Likely judge questions (2–3) | Closing line
```

## Performance-Indicator rule (non-negotiable in content and feedback rubrics)

⟨D6⟩ Saying "My first performance indicator is customer relations" earns nothing **on its own**. Every
PI must be **demonstrated** through: a concrete decision → business reasoning → implementation →
feasibility → measurement. All DECA feedback rubrics encode this; the AI judge/coach never rewards
PI-naming as a substitute for demonstration.

⟨B-2⟩ This rule is about **demonstration versus recitation**. It is **not** a ruling on whether to
speak a PI's title aloud — that remains an open judgment call (Module 1, L3).

## The five-part recommendation scaffold — CompeteReady's, not DECA's

⟨D7 — CRITICAL LABELING HONESTY⟩ **PROBLEM → RECOMMENDATION → BUSINESS REASON → IMPLEMENTATION →
MEASUREMENT is CompeteReady's teaching scaffold. It is not official DECA terminology.** It is designed
to map onto the official evaluation-form categories:

| Our scaffold step | Official anchor it serves |
|---|---|
| Problem | Correctly identifies the situation the PIs address |
| Recommendation | **Solution — Unique/Accurate** |
| Business reason | **Solution — Effective**; Career Competencies (reasoning) |
| Implementation | **Solution — Practical** |
| Measurement | **Solution — Practical / Effective** (testability) |

⟨B-8 — CONSTRAINED⟩ **Implementation and measurement are concrete ways to make a recommendation more
practical, testable, and aligned with the official Solution criteria. They may strengthen a response.
This research did not establish that they reliably separate top scores, and no score improvement is
promised.** Advisor/judge validation required.

⟨B-3 — OPTIONAL GLOSSARY CONTEXT ONLY⟩ Some competitors use **D-E-C-A-style mnemonics** for handling a
performance indicator. **Expansions differ between communities and none is official.** This is

scaffold remains separate and clearly labeled as ours; the community acronym is never assessed and

## Knowledge-to-Performance Bridge (business terms)

Every major business term eventually carries 7 fields, authored:

1. **Definition** (plain, current)
2. **Exam recognition** (how it's typically probed on the cluster exam)
3. **Scenario meaning** (what it looks like inside a role-play situation)
4. **What the character would DO** (the in-character decision)
5. **How the character EXPLAINS it to the judge** (one model sentence, natural speech — never the
   textbook definition recited)
6. **How it is implemented** (who/what/when)
7. **How success is measured** (the metrics)

Worked example (authoring standard):

> **TERM: Customer retention**
> Definition: keeping existing customers and encouraging repeat business.
> In-character use: "As the guest-experience manager, I would provide immediate service recovery and
> send a personalized follow-up offer. This addresses the current complaint while protecting the
> long-term relationship."
> Measurement: repeat booking rate, satisfaction scores, referral activity, offer redemption.

Drill type built on the bridge ("PI-to-action drill", doc 04): given a term or PI, the learner writes
the in-character sentence (fields 4–5) — not the definition.

### Customer Relations — scope and core definition

**Core definition (CompeteReady wording; concepts STABLE-TEACHING).**

> **Customer Relations is deciding what to say and do in a customer interaction, using the facts you
> actually have, the policy that applies, the options genuinely available, and the authority your role
> carries.**

Those four inputs — **facts · policy · options · authority** — are the spine of all six CR lessons.
**Many customer-relations decisions turn on one or more of them**, and they are a practical way to
check whether a response is grounded in the scenario rather than invented. **They are CompeteReady's
analytic model, not an exhaustive taxonomy** — tone, sequencing and acknowledgement are real parts
of an interaction that do not always reduce to one of four boxes.

⟨CR-A — SCENARIO FACTS CONTROL⟩ The employee may not invent policy, authority, remedies, customer
rights, or legal obligations. **None of the following is ever taught as a universal rule:** the
customer is always right · always refund · always replace · always compensate · always escalate ·
never escalate · override policy when the customer is upset · apologizing admits liability.

⟨CR-B — REJECTED AND NARROWED⟩ Of the concepts considered for this course, three were **narrowed** and
one was **rejected**. *Restoring trust* is taught as an outcome, not a technique. *Difficult
interactions* are taught only through observable behaviour. *Follow-up* is taught as situational, not
universal. **"Exceeding expectations" is rejected as a taught concept** — the idea of adding an
unrequested extra conflicts with ⟨CR-A⟩ unless the extra is itself authorized and available. See the
legacy-debt table for how `cr-09` is handled.

#### CR1 — What a customer-relations decision actually decides

- **Source tier:** STABLE-TEACHING.
- **Learning objective:** Given a customer situation, name which of the four inputs — facts, policy,
  options, authority — governs the response, and detect when a proposed response invents one.
- **Core concepts:** the four inputs; scenario facts control; the difference between *what the customer
  asked for* and *what is available*; why universal customer-service maxims fail as decision rules.
- **Learner decisions:** Which input is missing or unstated? Does this response assert a fact, policy,
  remedy, or authority the scenario never established? Is the request inside or outside what the role
  can do?
- **Worked example:** A customer asks for a full refund on a used item. The scenario states the store
  refunds unused items within 30 days and offers store credit outside that window; the employee may
  issue store credit without approval. The governing inputs are **policy** (credit, not refund) and
  **authority** (credit is within reach). A sound response states what is available and why, and does
  not promise the refund.
- **Non-example:** "The customer is upset, so I would give the refund to keep them happy." This invents
  authority the scenario did not grant and substitutes a maxim for a decision.
- **Common mistakes:** treating customer emotion as evidence about what policy allows; answering with a
  slogan; assuming a manager's powers; deciding before identifying which input is actually in question.
- **Boundary notes:** whether the refund is *worth it* to the business is **BR**. What a listed refund
  or service PI requires demonstrating is **PI**. This lesson is only the interaction decision.
- **Provenance notes:** the four-input framing is **CompeteReady synthesis**. Its authority component is
  grounded in the mainstream management definitions of authority, delegation, and accountability
  (OpenStax *Introduction to Business*, organizational-authority material).
- **Caveats:** ⟨BC-3⟩ applies in full — no legal entitlement is taught here.

#### CR2 — Understanding the concern before solving it

- **Source tier:** STABLE-TEACHING.
- **Learning objective:** Distinguish listening, paraphrasing, targeted clarification, and premature
  solution, and judge which is needed from the facts a scenario supplies.
- **Core concepts:**
  - **Listening** — understanding the customer's actual concern rather than the first thing said.
  - **Paraphrasing** — restating that concern accurately, in your own words, to confirm understanding.
  - **Targeted clarification** — asking a question **because a decision-relevant fact is missing**.
  - **Premature solution** — offering a remedy before the facts that determine the remedy are known.
- **Learner decisions:** Is a decision-relevant fact missing? Which single question would resolve it?
  Or are the facts already sufficient, making a question a delay rather than a service?
- **Worked example (clarification needed):** A customer says an online order "came wrong." Whether the
  remedy is a replacement, a return, or a correction depends on **what** is wrong — missing item, wrong
  item, or damaged item. One targeted question changes the available remedy, so it is asked first.
- **Worked example (clarification not needed):** The customer states the item arrived damaged, shows it,
  and the policy for damaged goods is stated in the scenario. Asking further questions to "show
  listening" delays a resolution that is already determined.
- **Non-example:** Repeating the customer's words verbatim and calling it paraphrasing. Paraphrase
  demonstrates understanding; echo demonstrates only hearing.
- **Common mistakes:** interrogating the customer for facts the scenario already gave; jumping to the
  most common remedy; treating clarification as a ritual required in every interaction.
- **Boundary notes:** ⟨CR-A⟩ — clarification gathers facts; it never manufactures them.
- **Provenance notes:** active listening and clarifying questions follow mainstream
  communication-education treatments (OpenStax *College Success*, communication chapter; OpenStax
  *Principles of Management*, management-communication channels). Paraphrase is defined there as
  restatement in one's own words to clarify meaning; **applying it to spoken service interactions is
  CompeteReady's extension of a written-communication definition, and is labeled as such.**
- **Caveats:** none beyond the paraphrase-transfer note above.

#### CR3 — Acknowledging without overpromising

- **Source tier:** STABLE-TEACHING.
- **Learning objective:** Separate acknowledging impact from claiming unestablished facts and from
  promising an unauthorized outcome.
- **Core concepts:** acknowledgement of impact; empathy as **attention to the customer**, not a script;
  the difference between "this was frustrating" (impact), "we made an error" (a fact claim), and "I'll
  refund you today" (an authority claim).
- **Learner decisions:** Is this statement acknowledging an experience or asserting a cause? Does it
  commit the business to an outcome the role can deliver?
- **Worked example:** "I understand this trip was wasted, and I want to get it sorted today. Let me
  confirm what happened with the order before I tell you what I can do." Acknowledges impact, commits
  to effort, commits to no outcome.
- **Non-example:** "I'm so sorry, that's completely our fault — I'll make sure you're compensated."
  Two invented commitments: a cause not established, and compensation not authorized.
- **Common mistakes:** treating an apology as a confession of cause; treating warmth as a substitute
  for a decision; using a memorized empathy phrase that does not fit the situation.
- **Boundary notes:** whether compensation is affordable or worthwhile is **BR**, not CR.
- **Provenance notes:** empathy is used in the service-quality sense — attentive focus on the customer
  so they receive caring, individual service — one of the five widely taught service-quality dimensions
  alongside reliability, assurance, tangibles, and responsiveness (OpenStax *Principles of Marketing*,
  service-quality chapter). **CompeteReady does not teach scripted empathy phrases**, and no
  emotionally manipulative wording is taught anywhere in this course.
- **Caveats:** the honesty rule from the Performance Course applies — a competitor never fabricates
  facts about the business to satisfy a customer.

#### CR4 — Explaining policy and offering real options

- **Source tier:** STABLE-TEACHING.
- **Learning objective:** Explain the applicable policy in plain language while stating what can
  actually be done, avoiding both the policy-only and the empathy-only failure.
- **Core concepts:** plain-language explanation; naming the option that exists; checking understanding
  when the explanation is complex; the two symmetrical failures:
  - **Policy-only failure** — quoting the rule accurately while ignoring the customer's actual concern.
  - **Empathy-only failure** — sounding supportive while offering something policy or authority does
    not permit.
- **Learner decisions:** What is the real option here? How is it said without jargon? Does the customer
  now know what happens next?
- **Worked example:** "That return window closed last week, so I can't process a refund. What I can do
  is exchange it for the same item or issue store credit today — which would you prefer?" The
  constraint is stated once, then the available options carry the response.
- **Non-example:** "Company policy is clear on returns." Accurate, complete, and useless — it names no
  option and leaves the customer without a next step.
- **Common mistakes:** hiding behind policy language; apologizing repeatedly instead of resolving;
  offering an option the scenario never made available; explaining the policy's history rather than its
  effect on this customer.
- **Boundary notes:** designing or changing the policy is **BR**. Applying it in this conversation is CR.
- **Provenance notes:** the plain-language and check-understanding elements follow mainstream
  business-communication teaching (OpenStax *Principles of Management*, communication chapter). The
  **policy-only / empathy-only failure pair is CompeteReady synthesis**, used because both failures are
  visible and correctable in a role-play.
- **Caveats:** ⟨BC-3⟩ — the scenario's stated policy is the only policy that exists.

#### CR5 — Service recovery when the business got it wrong

- **Source tier:** STABLE-TEACHING.
- **Learning objective:** Respond to a failure the business caused by establishing what happened,
  acknowledging impact, and delivering an authorized remedy — selecting the steps the situation calls
  for rather than reciting a sequence.
- **Core concepts:** service failure; recovery as a response to a failure the business is responsible
  for; why listening to complaints is treated in mainstream service teaching as information about the
  business, not merely as a problem to absorb.

⟨CR-C — CompeteReady teaching scaffold⟩ **CompeteReady service-recovery scaffold:**

1. Establish what actually happened.
2. Acknowledge the impact on the customer.
3. Identify what can actually be done.
4. Offer a remedy that policy and authority allow.
5. Explain the next step so the customer knows what happens now.
6. Escalate if the needed resolution exceeds your authority.
7. Follow through where a commitment was made or the resolution is incomplete.

> **This is a CompeteReady teaching scaffold, not official DECA terminology and not a scoring formula.
> It is not an external published framework. Use the parts that fit the scenario rather than reciting
> the sequence; the order changes with the facts, and several steps are frequently unnecessary.** No
> score improvement is claimed for using it.

- **Learner decisions:** Which steps does this failure actually require? Is the remedy authorized? Is
  step 6 triggered?
- **Worked example:** A prepaid order was never delivered; the scenario states the employee may
  re-ship at no charge. Steps 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5 apply; step 6 does not, because the remedy is within
  authority; step 7 applies because the re-ship is a commitment.
- **Non-example:** Walking a customer through all seven steps when the fix is immediate and complete —
  the scaffold becomes a performance rather than a resolution.
- **Common mistakes:** treating a fixed order as required; apologizing before knowing what happened,
  then contradicting the apology; naming a remedy before checking authority.
- **Boundary notes:** whether recovery costs are justified, and what recovery does to retention
  economics, is **BR**.
- **Provenance notes:** the *components* are mainstream — service failure, complaints as usable
  information, and the responsiveness dimension of service quality are standard in service-marketing
  teaching (OpenStax *Principles of Marketing*, service-quality chapter). **The seven-step arrangement
  is CompeteReady's and is labeled above.** Complaint handling is a defined organizational process in
  regulated settings, with expectations of complete, accurate, and timely response (U.S. CFPB
  consumer-complaint company-response program description) — **cited only to establish that complaint
  handling is a structured process; its sector-specific response deadlines are not taught and do not
  generalize.**
- **Caveats:** legacy `cr-01` and `cr-06` teach fixed sequences; this lesson deliberately does not.
  ISO 10002, the international complaints-handling guidance standard, is **named here as a known

#### CR6 — Authority, escalation, and follow-through

- **Source tier:** STABLE-TEACHING.
- **Learning objective:** Decide whether a situation requires escalation, and judge when follow-up is
  warranted — in both directions.
- **Core concepts:**
  - **Authority** — legitimate power granted by the organization to act and expect compliance;
    delegated down a chain of command, and paired with accountability for the outcome.
  - **Escalate when** the resolution the situation needs falls outside the employee's stated authority,
    or requires information or a decision only another role holds.
  - **Do not escalate when** the employee already has the information, the authority, and an available
    remedy. Reflexive escalation is a failure, not caution.
  - **Follow-through** — appropriate where a commitment was made, another person must act, the
    resolution is incomplete, or the situation is complex enough that confirming the fix matters. **Not
    required after every interaction.**
  - **Difficult interactions** — observable behaviour only: stay professional, do not argue, do not
    match hostility, gather the facts you need, explain the options, hold appropriate boundaries, and
    involve someone else when required.
- **Learner decisions:** Is the needed remedy inside my authority? Is escalation genuine or avoidance?
  Did I make a commitment that obliges follow-up?
- **Worked example (escalate):** The customer requests a refund beyond the amount the employee may
  authorize. The employee explains what they can do now, states plainly that the larger amount needs a
  manager, and brings the manager in rather than guessing.
- **Worked example (do not escalate):** The same store, an in-window exchange the employee is
  authorized to process. Fetching a manager wastes the customer's time and signals the employee does
  not know their own role.
- **Non-example:** "I'll have to check with my manager" used as a way to end an uncomfortable
  conversation the employee could resolve.
- **Common mistakes:** escalating to avoid discomfort; refusing to escalate to appear capable;
  promising follow-up with no mechanism to deliver it; treating an angry customer as automatically wrong.
- **Boundary notes:** **Safety is not a service skill.** Where a situation becomes threatening, the
  correct action is to disengage and involve the people and procedures the workplace designates.
  CompeteReady teaches no physical-confrontation technique and no method for managing a violent person.
- **Provenance notes:** authority, delegation, chain of command, and accountability follow mainstream
  management definitions (OpenStax *Introduction to Business*, organizational-authority material). The
  **two-direction escalation rule is CompeteReady synthesis** derived from those definitions. The safety
  boundary reflects public occupational-safety guidance that de-escalation and response to volatile
  situations belong to employer-established workplace-violence programs and training (U.S. OSHA
  workplace-violence prevention guidance) — **used only to keep this topic out of scope**, not to teach
  a technique.
- **Caveats:** legacy `cr-07` universalizes follow-up; this lesson does not.

### Marketing Fundamentals — scope and core definition

**Core definition (CompeteReady wording; concepts STABLE-TEACHING).**

> **Marketing Fundamentals is the set of basic concepts a business uses to connect what it offers with
> the customers it means to serve — who they are, what they value, how the offering is understood, and
> how it reaches them.**

⟨MK-A — REJECTED AND NARROWED⟩ *Simple competitive distinction* folds into differentiation and *basic
brand positioning* folds into positioning, rather than being taught as separate concepts.
**Market-research and feedback *measurement* is assigned to BR, not MK** — deployed BR already owns
metric selection, fit, interpretation, and collectability (`br-25`–`br-28`). Understanding *who the
customer is* stays in MK; judging whether a measurement is any good is BR.

#### MK1 — Who the customer is

- **Source tier:** STABLE-TEACHING.
- **Learning objective:** Distinguish a customer need, a market segment, and a target market, and
  identify which one a scenario is actually describing.
- **Core concepts:**
  - **Customer need** — what the customer is trying to get done or solve.
  - **Market segmentation** — dividing a market into smaller, more precisely defined groups whose
    members have common needs and can be expected to respond similarly to a marketing action.
  - **Target market** — the group the business **chooses to focus** its marketing effort and resources
    on, selected from the segments identified.
  - **Segmentation and targeting are not synonyms.** Segmentation describes the market; targeting is a
    decision about where to concentrate.
- **Learner decisions:** Is this scenario describing how the market divides, or which division the
  business has chosen? Does the proposed action follow from the chosen target?
- **Worked example:** A bike shop finds its buyers fall into commuters, weekend riders, and competitive
  cyclists — that is segmentation. Choosing to build the business around commuters is targeting, and it
  is the reason a commuter-focused service package is the coherent next move.
- **Non-example:** "Our target market is everyone who might buy a bike." That declines to target; it
  names the market, not a choice within it.
- **Common mistakes:** using the two terms interchangeably; defining a target market so broadly it
  excludes nobody; describing a segment and calling it a strategy.
- **Boundary notes:** whether the target market is *large enough to be worth serving* is a BR judgment
  once it turns on revenue, cost, or feasibility.
- **Provenance notes:** definitions follow OpenStax *Principles of Marketing* (segmentation and
  target-market chapter), where segmentation, targeting, and positioning are presented together as the
  major elements of a customer-driven marketing strategy. Target-market description is consistent with
  the U.S. Small Business Administration's public business guide on market research and competitive
  analysis.
- **Caveats:** **segmentation examples in this course use ordinary product and service characteristics
  only.** Scenarios based on sensitive personal characteristics are out of scope, and no lesson or drill
  may teach targeting that would exclude or single out people on such a basis.

#### MK2 — Why they would choose you

- **Source tier:** STABLE-TEACHING.
- **Learning objective:** Distinguish differentiation from a value proposition, and test whether a
  claimed difference actually matters to the intended customer.
- **Core concepts:**
  - **Differentiation** — how the offering meaningfully differs from the alternatives.
  - **Value proposition** — a concise statement of the benefits the intended customer can expect, and
    why the offering meets their needs better than the competition; in effect a promise to the customer.
  - **Customer value** — the relationship between the benefits the customer perceives and what it costs
    them to obtain the offering.
  - **Different does not mean better.** A difference is only useful if the target customer values it.
- **Learner decisions:** Is this a real difference? Does the target customer care about it? Does the
  value proposition name a benefit or only a feature?
- **Worked example:** A meal-kit service uses regional suppliers. That is a difference. It becomes a
  value proposition only when stated as the benefit the chosen customer wants — fresher ingredients
  with a shorter delivery window — rather than as a fact about the supply chain.
- **Non-example:** "We're the only ones who use a purple box." Distinct, verifiable, and irrelevant to
  the customer's decision.
- **Common mistakes:** listing features as value; claiming uniqueness the scenario does not support;
  writing a value proposition that would fit any competitor equally.
- **Boundary notes:** whether the differentiating choice is affordable or sustainable is **BR**.
- **Provenance notes:** value proposition and customer value follow OpenStax *Principles of Marketing*
  key-term definitions; differentiation follows the same text's treatment of differentiation as
  emphasizing an offering's unique qualities relative to the competition.
- **Caveats:** none.

#### MK3 — How you want to be understood

- **Source tier:** STABLE-TEACHING.
- **Learning objective:** Define positioning and keep it distinct from differentiation and from the
  value proposition.
- **Core concepts:**
  - **Positioning** — deciding and communicating how the business wants its market to think and feel
    about the product or service, relative to the alternatives.
  - The three-way distinction: **differentiation** is what is actually different · **positioning** is
    how the offering is meant to be understood · **value proposition** is why the intended customer
    should care.
  - **Price and quality cues as signals** — where a scenario supports it, price is one of the signals
    that communicates a position.
- **Learner decisions:** Is this scenario about a real difference, an intended perception, or a stated
  benefit? Do the business's choices support the position it claims?
- **Worked example:** A café priced above its neighbours, using single-origin beans and table service,
  is positioned as a premium experience; the price is part of the message, not only a revenue number.
- **Non-example:** A business claiming a premium position while competing on discount coupons — the
  signals contradict the claimed position.
- **Common mistakes:** collapsing all three terms into "branding"; treating positioning as a slogan;
  asserting a position the offering does not support.
- **Boundary notes:** whether the premium price is *profitable* is **BR**. Legacy `mk-07` sits here and
  is compatible.
- **Provenance notes:** positioning follows OpenStax *Principles of Marketing* (product-positioning
  chapter). The American Marketing Association's public materials likewise describe brand positioning
  as defining an organization's position relative to competitors and guiding communication of value —
  used as a **second, independent source** for the same concept, not as a rules source.
- **Caveats:** none.

#### MK4 — The offering and its price

- **Source tier:** STABLE-TEACHING.
- **Learning objective:** Explain product and price as marketing-mix levers, and hold the line between
  price as a marketing decision and price as a financial calculation.
- **Core concepts:**
  - **Marketing mix / the 4 Ps** — product, price, place, and promotion: the controllable tactics a
    business uses to bring an offering to a market.
  - **Product** — the good or service the business provides.
  - **Price** — what the customer pays in exchange for the product, and one of the clearest signals of
    intended position.
  - **The 4 Ps are mainstream marketing terminology, not a CompeteReady scaffold**, and not a complete
    answer to every marketing problem. **Recognizing which P a decision belongs to is a different skill
    from choosing well inside it.**
- **Learner decisions:** Which lever is this decision actually about? Is the question asking what price
  *communicates* or whether the price *works financially*?
- **Worked example:** Raising a subscription price while adding a service tier is a product-and-price
  decision that also changes position — a marketing question about what the offering now is and says.
- **Non-example:** "At $40 per unit with $22 of cost, the margin supports the discount." That is a
  financial judgment and belongs to Business Reasoning.
- **Common mistakes:** answering a pricing question with margin arithmetic; treating the 4 Ps as a
  checklist that resolves the scenario; naming the category and stopping there.
- **Boundary notes — the pricing boundary:** **MK** = what price is as a marketing lever and what it
  signals · **BR** = whether a specific price decision is justified, affordable, risky, or measurable ·
  **PI** = what a listed pricing competency requires demonstrating.
- **Provenance notes:** marketing-mix and 4 Ps definitions follow OpenStax *Principles of Marketing*
  (marketing-mix chapter).
- **Caveats:** none.

#### MK5 — Getting it to the customer

- **Source tier:** STABLE-TEACHING.
- **Learning objective:** Explain place/distribution and identify what drives a channel choice, without
  crossing into channel cost analysis.
- **Core concepts:**
  - **Place / distribution** — where and how the customer obtains the product; the path an offering
    takes from seller to buyer, including any intermediaries.
  - **Intermediaries** — businesses in the channel that help move products from producer to final
    customer, adding value through activities such as sorting, storing, and breaking bulk.
  - **Channel choice drivers** — target-market coverage, and what buyers require from the channel:
    information, convenience, variety, and pre- or post-sale service.
- **Learner decisions:** What does this customer need from the channel? Does the proposed channel reach
  the chosen target market? Is the scenario asking about access or about cost?
- **Worked example:** A specialty tool that buyers need demonstrated before purchase is a poor fit for a
  pure online-only channel — the buyer requirement is information, and the channel must supply it.
- **Non-example:** "We should use the cheaper distributor to protect margin." A cost decision, not a
  channel-fit decision.
- **Common mistakes:** equating distribution with shipping; ignoring what the buyer needs at the point
  of purchase; drifting into cost comparison.
- **Boundary notes:** channel **cost, ROI, and resource allocation are BR.** This lesson builds no
  allocation framework.
- **Provenance notes:** channel, intermediary, and channel-choice-factor definitions follow OpenStax
  *Principles of Marketing* (marketing-channels chapters, including factors influencing channel choice).
- **Caveats:** none.

#### MK6 — Telling them about it

- **Source tier:** STABLE-TEACHING.
- **Learning objective:** Explain promotion's purpose and judge audience–message–channel fit, without
  crossing into campaign measurement.
- **Core concepts:**
  - **Promotion** — the communication efforts a business uses to inform and persuade customers.
  - **Promotion mix** — advertising, sales promotion, personal selling, public relations, direct
    marketing, and digital/Internet marketing.
  - **Advertising** — paid communication identifying a brand or organization, intended to reach many
    recipients. **Sales promotion** — short-term inducement built around a direct call to action.
  - **Consistency** — coordinating promotional activity so the target audience receives one clear,
    consistent, customer-focused message.
  - **Audience–message–channel fit** — a strong promotion choice matches the message to the intended
    audience and uses a channel that can realistically reach that audience.
- **Learner decisions:** Who is this message for? Does the channel reach them? Does the message say
  something the target customer values?
- **Worked example:** A campus-focused service promoting through campus social accounts and student-org
  partnerships fits audience and channel; the same message in a regional newspaper does not.
- **Non-example:** "We tracked cost per acquisition and cut the underperforming channel." That is
  measurement and belongs to Business Reasoning.
- **Common mistakes:** picking a channel by popularity rather than audience; writing a promotion that
  restates the product without a customer benefit; sliding into metrics.
- **Boundary notes:** campaign ROI, metric selection, conversion measurement, and cost-per-acquisition
  analysis are **BR**. Legacy `mk-08` sits on the wrong side of this line — see the debt table.
- **Provenance notes:** promotion-mix, advertising, sales-promotion, and coordinated-communication
  definitions follow OpenStax *Principles of Marketing* (promotion chapters).
- **Caveats:** none.
