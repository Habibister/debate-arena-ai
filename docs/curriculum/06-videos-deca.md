## VIDEO 9 — What Happens in a DECA Role-Play

- **Objective:** the learner can describe the role-play sequence from scenario card to ballot and
  say what the judge is scoring.
- **Duration:** ~4 min. **Placement:** DECA Lesson 0.
- **Framing:** medium shot; insert shots of a scenario card and a prep sheet as props.

**Script (spoken):**

"A DECA role-play is a short business meeting that you perform. Here's the whole event, start to
finish, so your first one feels like your tenth.

[INSERT: hand receiving a scenario card]

It starts with a card. The card tells you three things — read them in this order: who YOU are, who
the JUDGE is playing, and the situation. You might be a front-desk manager; the judge might be an
upset guest, a store owner, your boss. From the first second, that's who you're both being.

The card also lists performance indicators — the specific skills you must demonstrate. Not mention.
Demonstrate. If a PI says 'handle customer complaints,' the judge needs to watch you actually handle
one.

[SCREEN: RECEIVE SCENARIO → IDENTIFY ROLES → IDENTIFY PROBLEM → READ PIs → PREPARE]

You get prep time — for the individual events it's ten minutes — with the card and blank paper.
Then you walk into the meeting, and here's the part nobody tells beginners: it's a conversation with
a person in character, not a presentation to a class.

[SCREEN: OPEN → RECOMMEND → JUSTIFY → IMPLEMENT → MEASURE → ANSWER QUESTIONS → CLOSE → BALLOT]

In the meeting you open in character, present specific recommendations, give the business reasons
they'd work, explain how you'd implement them and how you'd measure success.

[SCREEN: Present uninterrupted → then questions → then clarifications]

Here's a detail beginners get wrong: you present *uninterrupted*. The judge doesn't cut in mid-pitch.
Questions come after. In the individual series, principles, and personal financial literacy events,
those are **scripted standard questions — the same ones every competitor in your event gets** —
followed by any clarifications. In team decision making, questioning is conditional plus
clarifications. And in professional selling and consulting, there are **no scripted standard
questions at all; the judge may ask appropriate questions if time remains.**

Then you close, and the judge scores your event's official evaluation form — built on the performance
indicators plus professional skills like reasoning and communication.

[PAUSE — Q: The judge asks a tough question after your pitch. Is that a sign you're failing?]

No — questions are the event working as designed, and in most role-play families every competitor
gets the same scripted ones. Judges probe good ideas too. Collapsing under the question helps nobody;
defending your reasoning is the skill being tested.

One honest note: the exact evaluation-form categories and point values differ by event, by season, and
right now by which form generation your event uses — always check your event's current official guide
and form. What never changes: specific recommendations, real reasons, and staying in character beat
memorized vocabulary every single time."

**Practice prompt:** "Open Lesson 0. Read the practice scenario and identify: your role, the judge's
role, and the business problem — in under 90 seconds."
**Retake instruction:** "Missed one? Reread only the first three sentences of the card — role
information always lives there — and try the next card."
**Editing notes:** the two sequence graphics should persist as a growing timeline across the video;
end card includes the not-official disclaimer.

## VIDEO 10 — How to Use Preparation Time

- **Objective:** the learner can produce a complete DECA prep sheet in ten minutes using the
  standard layout.
- **Duration:** ~4.5 min. **Placement:** DECA Lesson 4.
- **Framing:** split: medium shot + persistent overhead insert of the sheet being written in real
  time.

**Script (spoken):**

"Ten minutes of prep decides most role-plays before they start. Most beginners spend it wrong —
scripting their opening sentence over and over, or writing so much they can't find anything. Here's
what to do instead, on a real sheet, in real time.

[SCREEN: 0–2 min read and mark the card · 2–4 min problem + PIs as actions · 4–8 min two
recommendations with reason, implementation, measurement · 8–10 min opening, likely questions, close]

A budget first, because time is the thing that actually runs out. Roughly: two minutes reading and
marking the card. Two more pinning the problem down and turning the performance indicators into
actions. Four minutes building two recommendations. The last two on your opening, your likely
questions, and your close. Adjust it to suit you — but have one, because 'I'll just work through it'
is how people arrive at minute nine with no closing line.

The second rule matters as much: **write cues, not sentences.** Three words per performance indicator,
not a paragraph. You cannot read an essay while maintaining a conversation, and every word you write
past a cue is a word you'll never look at again.

[OVERHEAD: blank page; the layout gets drawn as it's described]

The layout — same one every single time, so it's automatic under pressure.

Top of the page: my role, the judge's role, and the main problem in ONE sentence. If you can't write
the problem in one sentence, you haven't found it yet — keep reading the card.

Left margin: the performance indicators, but rewritten as actions. Not 'customer relations' — 
'SHOW me recovering this customer.'

Center: recommendation one. Under it, three short lines: the business reason, the implementation,
the measurement. Right side: recommendation two, same three lines.

Bottom: my opening line, two or three questions the judge will probably ask, and my closing line.

[PAUSE — Q: Why write the judge's likely questions during prep instead of hoping they don't come?]

Because the questions are predictable! Cost, feasibility, 'why not the alternative' — if you've
written them down, the scariest part of the event becomes the part you rehearsed.

Watch the fill-in on a real card: hotel, overbooked suite, upset loyalty guest.

[OVERHEAD: sheet filling in, ~60 seconds, sped up 2x with timestamps]

Top: front-desk manager / the guest / 'a loyalty guest's promised suite is gone — retention risk.'
PIs as actions: 'handle THIS complaint', 'show service drives retention.'
Rec 1: upgrade plus anniversary recovery tonight — reason: retention economics — implementation:
15 minutes, personal follow-up — measure: repeat booking.
Rec 2: loyalty-tier room buffer — reason: protect highest-value guests — implementation: pilot one
quarter — measure: loyalty complaint rate.
Bottom: opening line, the cost question, the closing line.

That's a complete sheet with minutes left over — and notice what I never wrote: full sentences.
The sheet is a spine, not a script. You sound like a manager when you talk from a spine; you sound
like a student when you read a script."

**Practice prompt:** "Lesson 4 gives you a fresh card and a 10-minute timer. Build the sheet with
the layout. The rubric checks all six zones."
**Retake instruction:** "Over ten minutes? Redo the SAME card — your second sheet on the same
scenario should take half the time. That speed is the skill."
**Editing notes:** overhead insert must remain readable (dark ink, good light); show a final still
of the completed sheet for 5 full seconds; end card disclaimer.

## VIDEO 11 — Turning Performance Indicators into Natural Speech

- **Objective:** the learner can convert a PI from a label into an in-character demonstrated action
  with a reason and a metric.
- **Duration:** ~4 min. **Placement:** DECA Lesson 3.
- **Framing:** medium shot; PI method graphic persistent during demos.

**Script (spoken):**

"Here's the single most common way DECA beginners lose points. They walk in and say:

'My first performance indicator is customer relations. Customer relations means maintaining
positive relationships with customers. It is very important for business.'

That's a vocabulary quiz answer wearing a suit. It scores almost nothing, because the judge isn't
scoring whether you can define the PI. They're scoring whether you can DO it.

[SCREEN: PI → PLAIN MEANING → SCENARIO REQUIREMENT → IN-CHARACTER ACTION → JUDGE-FACING
EXPLANATION → MEASUREMENT]

The conversion method. Take the PI 'handle customer complaints.'

Plain meaning: when a customer is unhappy, fix it well.
Scenario requirement: in our hotel scenario, the complaint is a lost anniversary suite — so the PI
means: recover THIS guest, tonight.
In-character action — and this is where the points live. Not the definition. The action:

'Mrs. Rivera, we failed to hold your suite, and that's on us. Here's what I'm doing right now: I'm
moving you to the Penthouse Corner suite at no charge, with an anniversary setup, and housekeeping
will have it ready in fifteen minutes.'

[PAUSE — Q: Did that response ever say the words 'customer complaints'? Did it demonstrate the PI?]

Never said it. Fully demonstrated it. That's the standard: the judge should be able to check the PI
box without you ever naming the PI.

Then the explanation — one sentence of business logic, in character: 'A recovered guest stays loyal,
and keeping a loyalty member costs far less than winning a new one.' And the measurement: 'I'd watch
repeat booking and satisfaction scores this quarter.'

Do that chain for every PI on your card and you're not reciting knowledge anymore — you're running a
business meeting. Which is the event."

**Practice prompt:** "Lesson 3 hands you a PI and a scenario. Write the in-character action sentence
and the one-line business reason. The rubric rejects definition-reciting."
**Retake instruction:** "If your sentence contains the PI's name, that's the tell — rewrite it so
the judge could still check the box."
**Editing notes:** weak PI-recital demo gets the desaturated small-frame treatment; keep the 6-step
chain graphic persistent from its introduction onward; end card disclaimer.

## VIDEO 12 — Business Terminology in Character

- **Objective:** the learner can deploy a business term inside natural in-character speech instead
  of defining it.
- **Duration:** ~3.5 min. **Placement:** DECA Knowledge-to-Performance Bridge intro (linked from
  Lessons 3 and 9).
- **Framing:** medium shot.

**Script (spoken):**

"Knowing a business term and USING a business term are different skills. The exam tests the first.
The role-play scores the second. Watch the difference on one term: customer retention.

Definition mode — knowledge, no performance:

'Customer retention refers to the ability of a company to retain its customers over time and
encourage repeat business.'

Fine for the exam. In a role-play, that sentence is filler. Now character mode:

'As the guest-experience manager, my priority is the relationship, not just tonight. I'd resolve the
complaint immediately, then follow up with a personalized return offer — because keeping a guest we
already won is far cheaper than winning a new one, and I'd expect to see it in our repeat-booking
rate.'

[PAUSE — Q: The second version never defined retention. What did it do with the concept instead?]

It made a decision with it. That's the pattern for every term you know:

[SCREEN: DEFINITION → what would my CHARACTER DO with this? → how would they EXPLAIN it to the
judge? → how would they MEASURE it?]

Try one more: 'market segmentation.' Definition mode says 'dividing a market into groups with
shared characteristics.' Character mode — you're a hotel marketing manager:

'Our guests aren't one audience — business travelers midweek, anniversary couples on weekends. I'd
run separate offers for each: express check-in bundles for the business segment, experience packages
for couples — and track conversion by segment so we know which message is earning its budget.'

The concept is doing work now. Every term in your study decks has this second life — the app's
bridge drills make you write it. Speak the language like an owner, not like a glossary."

**Practice prompt:** "Bridge drill: you get a term you've already studied. Write the character
sentence — decision, judge-facing reason, metric. No definitions allowed."
**Retake instruction:** "If your answer starts with '[Term] means…' — that's definition mode.
Restart the sentence with 'I would…'."
**Editing notes:** definition-mode reads should be visually flat (static frame); character-mode
delivered with full engagement; end card disclaimer.

## VIDEO 13 — Weak Versus Strong DECA Recommendation

- **Objective:** the learner can identify the five components of a complete recommendation and
  upgrade a vague one.
- **Duration:** ~4 min. **Placement:** DECA Lesson 8 (pairs with 9–12).
- **Framing:** medium shot; five-part structure graphic builds during the strong demo.

**Script (spoken):**

"Same scenario, two recommendations. You're the front-desk manager; the judge is a guest whose
promised anniversary suite was overbooked away.

Weak recommendation — and listen closely, because it SOUNDS okay:

'I would definitely apologize to the guest and try to find her another room, maybe offer some kind
of discount, and we'd work on doing better with overbooking in the future. We really value her
business.'

[PAUSE — Q: It was polite and it wasn't wrong. Name one thing a judge could actually score in it.]

Almost nothing. 'Try to find', 'maybe some kind of', 'work on doing better' — no specific action, no
reason, no plan, no number. Politeness is not a recommendation.

Strong recommendation, same moment:

[SCREEN: builds line by line — PROBLEM → RECOMMENDATION → BUSINESS REASON → IMPLEMENTATION →
MEASUREMENT]

'We failed a loyalty guest on a promised suite — that's a retention risk, not just a bad night.
(PROBLEM)
Tonight: I'm upgrading her to the Penthouse Corner suite at no charge with an anniversary setup.
(RECOMMENDATION — specific, tonight, named.)
A recovered guest stays loyal, and retaining a loyalty member costs a fraction of acquiring a new
one. (BUSINESS REASON)
Housekeeping turns the suite in fifteen minutes; I follow up personally at checkout. (IMPLEMENTATION
— who and when.)
And going forward I'd hold a small buffer of loyalty-tier suites, piloted for a quarter, measured by
loyalty-guest complaint rate and repeat-booking rate. (MEASUREMENT — the numbers that prove it
worked.)'

Five components, every time: problem, recommendation, reason, implementation, measurement. Beginners
stop at two. The gap between 'try to find her a room' and that full chain is the gap between the
middle of the pack and the stage."

**Practice prompt:** "Lesson 8 shows you a vague recommendation. Upgrade it: keep the same idea, add
the missing components. The rubric checks all five."
**Retake instruction:** "Read your upgrade aloud as the manager. Any sentence with 'try to' or
'maybe' gets rewritten as a decision."
**Editing notes:** the five-part graphic must lock in as a persistent checklist after the strong
demo; end card disclaimer.

## VIDEO 14 — Answering Judge Questions

- **Objective:** the learner can answer a skeptical judge question with the direct-answer frame and
  without abandoning their recommendation.
- **Duration:** ~4 min. **Placement:** DECA Lesson 14.
- **Framing:** presenter answers questions read by an off-camera voice (the "judge"); single camera.

**Script (spoken):**

"After you present — uninterrupted — the judge asks questions. This is the part of the event that
beginners fear most and top competitors love most, because it's where prepared people separate from
memorized people.

[SCREEN: Series / Principles / PFL — scripted standard questions, same for everyone · TDM —
conditional questioning + clarifications · PSC — no scripted questions; judge may ask if time remains]

Know which applies to your event. In the individual series, principles, and personal financial
literacy events the standard questions are **scripted and identical for every competitor** — which
means you can prepare for them directly. Team decision making uses conditional questioning plus
clarifications. Professional selling and consulting has **no scripted standard questions; the judge
may ask appropriate questions if time remains** — so don't build your plan around a question round
that may not happen.

The frame is three moves:

[SCREEN: DIRECT ANSWER → REASON → TIE BACK TO YOUR RECOMMENDATION]

Question one — the cost attack:

(off-screen) 'A room buffer means empty rooms. Why would I accept that as the owner?'

Collapse response — what fear sounds like:

'Um, yes, that's true, it would cost money… I guess we don't have to do the buffer part…'

That's abandoning your plan at the first push. The judge just watched your recommendation die.

Same question, the frame:

'Because the math favors it. (DIRECT ANSWER — first sentence answers the question.)
A handful of unsold room-nights costs less than losing a loyalty guest — with her lifetime value and
her referrals — to a preventable failure. (REASON — the business logic.)
That's why I'd pilot it for one quarter and let the repeat-booking numbers make the case before we
commit hotel-wide. (TIE BACK — the recommendation survives, now with proof attached.)'

[PAUSE — Q: The strong answer conceded that the buffer costs money. Why did that help instead of
hurt?]

Because the judge already knows it costs money. Owning the cost and weighing it makes you credible;
pretending there's no cost makes you a salesperson.

One more question type — the curveball: 'What if the guest posts the bad experience online tonight
before you fix it?' You won't have prepped every question, and that's fine. The frame still holds:
direct answer first — 'Then our response becomes public too, so I'd make the recovery visible' —
reason, tie back. A calm structure beats a memorized answer every time.

[SCREEN: Don't know? → honest answer → how you'd find out → the follow-up]

And the question you genuinely cannot answer. There's a popular piece of advice that says make
something up and say it confidently. Don't. Inventing a number or a policy is the one answer that can
fall apart under a single follow-up — and business people ask follow-ups. Do this instead: say what you
do know, name how you'd find the rest, and offer the next step. 'I don't have our current no-show rate
in front of me. I'd pull the last two quarters from the property-management system before committing to
a buffer size, and I can have that to you Monday.' That is a competent professional answer. It is also
true, which the invented one isn't.

Never do these: never argue with the judge's premise angrily, never say 'that's not part of my
plan,' never invent facts to fill a silence, and never — ever — go quiet and wait for rescue. Direct
answer, reason, tie back."

**Practice prompt:** "Lesson 14 fires two questions at your Lesson-8 recommendation — one cost, one
curveball. Answer both with the frame."
**Retake instruction:** "If your first sentence didn't answer the question, redo just that answer —
start with 'Because' or 'Yes/No' to force directness."
**Editing notes:** off-camera judge voice should sound skeptical but professional, never hostile;
collapse demo max 7 seconds; end card disclaimer.
