## What it is

> A DECA role-play is a short business meeting you perform out loud. You are handed a business
> situation, given a few minutes to prepare, and then you meet a judge who is playing a specific
> character — your manager, a client, a franchise owner. Your job is to walk in, understand their
> problem, and recommend what the business should do, out loud, as if it were really happening.
>
> It is NOT a speech and it is NOT a quiz. You are not reciting terms. You are advising a person,
> in character, and defending your advice when they push back. By the end of this lesson you'll
> know every step, the exact structure of a strong recommendation, and what the judge is actually
> scoring.

### Key ideas

> - **Preparation time** — ⟨CHANGED⟩ For Individual Series events, you get 10 minutes with the
>   scenario before you meet the judge (per the current official guide — see the attribution on
>   this page). You use it to find the problem and plan your recommendations — not to memorize a
>   script.
> - **The participant (you)** — The role you are told to play — e.g. the front-desk manager.
>   Everything you say should sound like that person doing that job, not like a student giving a
>   presentation.
> - **The judge's role** — The judge plays an assigned character (a guest, an owner, your boss).
>   Speak TO that character. Read who they are before you plan what to say.
>   ⟨NEW — B-6⟩ One honest note: judges are volunteers. Some are experienced business people; some are
>   judging for the first time; some are tired at the end of a long day. Treat "business executive" as
>   a useful model, not a promise — and build a meeting that still lands with someone who isn't
>   tracking every word.
> - **Performance indicators (PIs)** — The specific skills the scenario says you must
>   demonstrate — e.g. "handle guest complaints." ⟨CHANGED — D6/D11⟩ You must SHOW each one in action,
>   not just name it. **Individual Series role-plays list five.** They are not announced in advance;
>   they're on the card.
> - **The score sheet** — ⟨CHANGED — D16⟩ Your event's current official evaluation form is the
>   authority. Role-play events score how well you demonstrate the required performance indicators,
>   plus a **Solution** judgement — is your answer accurate and original, is it *practical*, would it
>   be *effective* — and career-competency skills like reasoning and communication. (Individual Series
>   events also include a separate 100-question cluster exam that counts toward your standing.) Exact
>   categories and point values vary by event, by season, and right now by which form generation your
>   event uses. You win by demonstrating the PIs and defending real recommendations.

## The event, start to finish

> 1. Receive the scenario card → 2. Identify your role → 3. Identify the judge's assigned role →
> 4. Find the central business problem → 5. Read the performance indicators → 6. Use preparation
> time to plan recommendations → 7. Enter character and open professionally → 8. Present specific
> recommendations → 9. Explain why each works (business reasoning) → 10. Cover implementation, cost,
> and how you'd measure success → 11. **Present uninterrupted, then answer the judge's questions** →
> 12. Close persuasively → 13. Receive your score sheet

⟨NEW — D4⟩ **When the questions come, and which ones:**

> You present without being interrupted. Questions come after — and which kind depends on your event
> family:
>
> - **Individual Series, Principles, Personal Financial Literacy** — **scripted standard questions,
>   the same for every competitor in your event**, then any clarifications. You can prepare for these
>   directly.
> - **Team Decision Making** — conditional questioning, plus clarifications.
> - **Professional Selling and Consulting** — **no scripted standard questions; the judge may ask
>   appropriate questions if time remains.** Don't assume a question round will happen.

## The recommendation structure — CompeteReady's scaffold

⟨CHANGED — D7, labeling honesty⟩

> **This five-part shape is how CompeteReady teaches recommendations. It is not official DECA
> terminology** — you won't find these five words on an evaluation form. We use it because it lines
> up with what the official form actually judges.
>
> 1. **Problem** — name the business problem in one sentence
> 2. **Recommendation** — say exactly what you'd do → *the form's* **Solution: accurate and original**
> 3. **Business reason** — why it works (revenue, loyalty, cost, brand) → **Solution: effective**
> 4. **Implementation** — who does what, and by when → **Solution: practical**
> 5. **Measurement** — the number you'd watch to know it worked → **practical and testable**
>
> Every recommendation you make should hit all five. Beginners stop after "Recommendation."
>
> ⟨NEW — B-8, constrained⟩ Implementation and measurement are concrete ways to make a recommendation
> more practical and testable, and they line up with what the official form asks. **They may make your
> answer stronger. We can't tell you they'll raise your score — we haven't established that, and we're
> not going to promise it.**

⟨NEW — B-3, optional glossary context only⟩

> **You may hear other competitors use a D-E-C-A mnemonic** for handling a performance indicator
> (define it, explain it, connect it, apply it — or some other expansion). **The expansions differ from
> place to place and none of them is official.** It's here so the term isn't unfamiliar. Our five-part
> scaffold above is separate, it's ours, and it's what your practice feedback is built on. **You are
> never assessed on the acronym.**

## Scenario: the overbooked suite

> You are the front-desk manager at a busy hotel. A loyalty-program guest reserved a specific suite
> months ago for an anniversary, but the hotel overbooked and the suite is no longer available
> tonight. The judge is playing that guest, who has just arrived and is upset. Recommend how to
> handle this guest AND how to reduce overbooking problems going forward. Performance indicators:
> handle a guest complaint; explain the role of customer service in retention.

### What a 10-minute prep looks like — the prep sheet  ⟨CHANGED — B-1: adds a budget and a cue rule⟩

> **Budget the ten minutes before you use them.** Roughly:
>
> | Minutes | What you're doing |
> |---|---|
> | 0–2 | Read and mark the card |
> | 2–4 | Pin the problem in one sentence; turn the PIs into actions |
> | 4–8 | Build two recommendations, each with reason, implementation, metric |
> | 8–10 | Opening line, likely questions, closing line |
>
> Adjust it to suit you — but *have* one. Arriving at minute nine with no closing line is the
> commonest way to lose a prep period.
>
> **Write cues, not sentences.** About three words per performance indicator. You cannot read an essay
> while holding a conversation, and every word past a cue is a word you'll never look at again. Note
> bloat — not misunderstanding the scenario — is what usually goes wrong here.
>
> Build the same sheet every time, so it's automatic under pressure:
>
> - **TOP:** my role · the judge's role · the problem in ONE sentence
>   *(Front-desk manager / the upset anniversary guest / "a loyalty guest's promised suite is
>   gone — a service failure that threatens retention.")*
> - **LEFT:** the PIs, rewritten as actions to demonstrate — **cue words only**
>   *("recover THIS guest" · "service → retention")*
> - **CENTER:** Recommendation 1 (tonight): recover the guest — acknowledge, upgrade + comp, make
>   the anniversary special → reason → implementation → metric
> - **RIGHT:** Recommendation 2 (going forward): fix the cause — an overbooking buffer for loyalty
>   reservations → reason → implementation → metric
> - **BOTTOM:** my opening line · 2–3 questions the judge will probably ask · my closing line
>
> The sheet is a spine, not a script. You sound like a manager when you talk from a spine; you
> sound like a student when you read a script.
>
> ⟨NEW — D19⟩ Materials are provided at the event, and the notes you make during preparation are
> yours to use during the meeting. Check your event's current guide for exactly what's allowed —
> and note that **role-play events do not let you bring visual aids in.**
